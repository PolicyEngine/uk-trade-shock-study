\section{Introduction}\label{sec:intro}

Tax--benefit systems insure households against earnings losses
automatically: when pay falls, tax liabilities fall and means-tested
entitlements rise, with no decision by anyone. They do not insure
against import-price rises in the same way. Benefits are set in cash
and re-indexed once a year to past inflation, so when the price of
food or energy jumps, any support within the year has to be
legislated. Between 2021 and 2026 the United Kingdom met both kinds of
shock in quick succession: a new customs border with the EU, the 2022
terms-of-trade deterioration, US tariffs on its exports and a trade
agreement with India.

This paper asks who bore these four shocks and how much of their cost
the state cushioned, automatically and by discretion. I take the size
of each shock from published sources (an estimate for the post-Brexit
food-price effect, Ofgem's price-cap levels for energy, the
government's projection for the UK--India agreement) and build an
assumed earnings scenario for the tariffs. I then compute their
incidence by income decile, first on published expenditure tables and
then on household microdata in the PolicyEngine UK tax--benefit model,
where each legislated instrument and a counterfactual April 2022
uprating are scored on the same households. The results are
conditional incidence, given the shock, not causal estimates of what
each event did. For the energy episode the levels depend on two
conventions, the path of the price cap that the Energy Price Guarantee (the Guarantee) replaced
and the rebasing of the microdata to 2021--22 prices; I base the headline on the caps Ofgem actually set, weighted by seasonal
consumption, and report every rate across the alternatives.

There are four findings. First, both adverse price shocks were
regressive, because of the goods they fell on. On its realised path
the energy shock cost the bottom decile
\MsEnergyRealisedPctSpendDecOne\ per cent of spending against
\MsEnergyRealisedPctSpendDecTen\ per cent at the top, and the food
shock \MsTcaWindowAvgPctSpendDecOne\ against
\MsTcaWindowAvgPctSpendDecTen\ per cent; the UK--India agreement's
consumer-price gains fall on clothing and footwear and are small and
proportional. Yet \SsWithinPct\ per cent of the variance in the energy
burden lies within income deciles.

Second, the discretionary response to the energy shock was large and
the response to the food shock was nil. The Energy Price Guarantee
absorbed \SsEpgRatePct\ per cent of the counterfactual energy cost;
energy-specific instruments together returned
\SsEnergySpecificRatePct\ per cent
(\SsEnergySpecificRateMinPct--\SsEnergySpecificRateMaxPct\ across
price-path conventions) and, with the Cost of Living Payments,
\SsDiscretionaryRatePct\ per cent
(\SsFullRateMinPct--\SsFullRateMaxPct). On the full stack rates fall with
income above the third decile, whose point estimate is
\SsGridPaperFullMaxDecilePct\ per cent. No instrument was legislated
against the food-price rise.

Third, the one automatic price-side channel, indexation, arrived a
year late. Had the April 2022 uprating matched that year's inflation,
benefit income would have been \pounds\SsLagReformBn\ billion higher,
\pounds\SsLagDecAllGbp\ a household against \pounds\SsLagDecAllColGbp\
of Cost of Living Payments; the shortfall exceeded the payments in
every decile, narrowly in the bottom one.

Fourth, in the tariff scenario taxes and benefits offset
\EThreeDispTaxBenRatePct\ to \EThreeWageTaxBenRatePct\ per cent of
lost earnings. That rate is a property of the UK system, not of the
tariff: the same loss spread over all employees in proportion to earnings is cushioned
within a point of it. Who bears it is also largely a property of
earnings losses: as a share of household income the loss is close to
zero in the bottom decile (\EThreeDispSharePctDecOne\ per cent) and
falls on the middle and top of the distribution, where the price
shocks fell on the bottom. Against the uniform loss the tariff is
lighter still in the bottom decile.

The paper joins two literatures. Studies of trade and households
measure exposure through spending and earnings but stop before the
tax--benefit system \citep{porto2006, fajgelbaumkhandelwal2016}.
Studies of automatic stabilisers measure the system against income
and employment shocks \citep{dolls2012, brewertasseva2021}. The
closest work on the 2022 episode scores fiscal measures against
inflation in the EU \citep{amores2025} and UK tax--benefit policy over
2019--23 \citep{popova2026}, and \citet{levell2025} study the welfare
effects of the energy shock and its relief on transaction data. I add
three things: a decomposition of the energy response by instrument
and decile that separates energy-specific from general support; the
indexation lag by decile on the same households; and a comparison
across episodes that sets a cushioned price shock beside an
uncushioned one and an earnings shock beside both. The comparison is
descriptive. It cannot rank the episodes on welfare, and it does not
explain why policy responded to one price shock and not the other; it
records that it did, and what the difference was worth to households
at each point of the income distribution.

The estimates are first-round and static, they inherit the
uncertainty of the shocks they condition on, and the energy levels
depend on stated conventions that I vary. Section~\ref{sec:literature}
reviews the literature. Section~\ref{sec:framework} sets out the
framework, the four episodes and the method.
Section~\ref{sec:results} reports incidence on published expenditure
tables and Section~\ref{sec:results-secondstage} the household-level
results. Section~\ref{sec:discussion} concludes.
