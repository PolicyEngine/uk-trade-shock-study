\section{Results}\label{sec:results}

All results use FRS 2024--25 and period 2026. The bounds table uses
\SubmissionDraws\ balanced assignments; legacy secondary scenarios use
\ProductionDraws. Values after $\pm$ are assignment standard deviations, not
standard errors or confidence intervals. Numerical Monte Carlo SE is stored
separately in each JSON artifact. Five-assignment diagnostics are exploratory.

\subsection{Derived sector shocks}\label{sec:results-shocks}

The deterministic OBR-style lower-anchor stress is
\pounds\OBRLowWageGross\ million annually. The unit-stress full-schedule
amount is \pounds\FullWageGross\ million and the EPD unit stress is
\pounds\EPDWageGross\ million. These are scenario inputs generated by
Equation~\eqref{eq:shock}, not estimated causal effects. The unit-stress
sector rates are exactly half those of the former $\varepsilon=2$ high case
and 2.5 times the OBR-style rates.

\emph{Estimator provenance.} The cushioning levels and the gap in the
abstract and in this section come from one 50-assignment paired-draw design;
the wedge and offset quoted alongside them do not, and come from the
\FactorialChannelSeeds-seed channel split described below. Specifically: the wage-cut
and displacement cushioning levels are draw means of the two unit 12-month
scenarios, and the \PrimaryCushionDifference-point gap is the mean of the
per-draw differences on paired seeds, so the displayed levels and gap are
consistent up to rounding of the unrounded underlying estimates. The
record-resampling contrast reported below
(\BootstrapCushionDifference\ points) is a different estimator---the
draw-averaged contrast over the \BootstrapDraws\ common assignment draws
retained in the resampling cache---and the worker-selection family runs on a
third, the \LFSSelectionDraws-draw Bernoulli design, whose own baseline gap
is \LFSSelectionBaselineGap\ points. Three estimators of one contrast is two
too many for comfortable reading, so the convention is: the
\PrimaryCushionDifference-point figure is primary and every other is labelled
at the point of use. None of the three differs from another by as much as the
structural sensitivities reported below, which is the more important fact
about all of them.

\subsection{Factorial decomposition of the gap}\label{sec:results-factorial}

The two headline scenarios differ in two respects at once: the adjustment
margin and the concentration and identity of the losers. A diffuse wage cut
spreads the loss over all exposed workers; displacement concentrates it on
roughly ten selected survey records. To separate the channels, a factorial
middle cell---a \emph{concentrated wage cut}---imposes the displacement
draw's exact worker-level losses (the same balanced draw on the same seeds,
each drawn worker losing their entire annual earnings, with pension
contributions and statutory pay scaling to zero exactly as for any in-work
earnings change) while leaving every worker employed at baseline hours.
Because employment is binary, a ``diffuse displacement'' cell is not
definable, so the sequential path diffuse $\rightarrow$ concentrated
$\rightarrow$ displaced is the only feasible decomposition of the gap. No
axiomatic backing is claimed for it: a Shapley allocation over the two
factors would require the undefined diffuse-displacement cell to be valued
(Section~\ref{sec:method-margins}).

At the unit 12-month stress over \FactorialDraws\ paired assignments, the
concentrated wage cut is cushioned at \FactorialConcCushion\ per cent,
almost exactly the displacement rate (\FactorialDispCushion) and far below
the diffuse wage cut (\FactorialWageCushion). The decomposition is
one-sided: of the total $\FactorialGapTotal\pm\FactorialGapTotalSD$-point
gap, $\FactorialConcStep\pm\FactorialConcStepSD$ points arise from
concentration and worker selection while employment state is held fixed,
and only $\FactorialStateStep\pm\FactorialStateStepSD$ points---about
\FactorialStateShare\ per cent of the gap---from the employment-state
change itself, holding the loss-bearing workers and their worker-level
losses fixed. At the OBR-style anchor the pattern repeats (concentration
\FactorialOBRConcStep\ points, employment state
\FactorialOBRStateStep\ points). The channel split on common seeds locates
the concentration step almost entirely in the income-tax schedule: diffuse
cuts retain \FactorialConcStepIncomeTax\ points more marginal-rate
income-tax relief than the concentrated cell, partially offset by
pound-for-pound stronger Universal Credit support of the concentrated
losses, worth \FactorialConcStepUC\ points on that channel.
The employment-state step is nearly empty in every channel
(UC \FactorialStateStepUC\ points; income tax and National Insurance zero
by construction, since both cells zero the same earnings).

\paragraph{The analytic benchmark, and what the simulation adds.} The
direction and rough size of the concentration step are predictable from the
tax schedule alone, and the paper is stronger for saying so before claiming
a discovery. Consider a worker with earnings $E$ facing an effective
marginal deduction rate $m(E)$ and an average deduction rate $a(E)$ over the
whole of their earnings. A small diffuse cut removes income at the margin,
so the share of the gross loss absorbed by the state is $m(E)$---an effective
marginal rate. A complete loss removes every slice of the schedule, so the
absorbed share is a participation rate: $[T(E)-B_{\text{in}}+B_{\text{out}}]/E$,
the average tax rate less in-work entitlement forgone plus out-of-work
entitlement gained. It reduces to $a(E)$ only where out-of-work entitlement
is zero, which is the case the log-linear parameterisation of
Section~\ref{sec:lit-progressivity} describes and the United Kingdom does
not. The $B_{\text{out}}$ term is where the means-tested system enters, and
it is why the realised step falls short of the schedule's own wedge. Under any progressive schedule with a personal
allowance, $m(E)\ge a(E)$---strictly above the allowance, with equality below
it where both are zero---and the wedge $m(E)-a(E)$ is exactly the
concentration effect. It is also a familiar object: in the log-linear tax
function of \citet{heathcote2017} the progressivity parameter $\tau$
satisfies $1-m=(1-\tau)(1-a)$, so $m-a=\tau(1-a)$. The wedge is therefore
$\tau$ scaled down by one minus the average rate---at the weighted rates
below, a \ScheduleWedgeWeighted-point wedge corresponds to $\tau\approx0.20$,
not $0.16$. And it is the \emph{difference} between the two cushioning rates
that estimates the wedge, not either rate on its own: the diffuse rate
estimates the weighted marginal deduction rate, the concentrated rate the
average (Section~\ref{sec:lit-progressivity}). It is large for a worker whose earnings straddle the
higher-rate threshold, and it vanishes for a worker taxed at a single rate
from the first pound.

The benchmark is worth making numerical, because it can then be checked
directly. For a single worker on the exposed-sector mean of
\pounds 48{,}272 under the 2026 schedule, income tax and employee National
Insurance take \ScheduleMarginalRate\ per cent of a marginal pound but only
\ScheduleAverageRate\ per cent of the whole year's earnings, because zeroing
the year also removes the untaxed personal allowance---a wedge of
\ScheduleImpliedGap\ percentage points.

That single-worker figure is the wrong weight for the aggregate being
compared, and correcting the weight is informative---but the corrected
quantity is not an independent prediction, and earlier drafts presented it as
one. The comparison is wage-bill-weighted over a population more concentrated
above the higher-rate and upper-earnings-limit thresholds than a worker on
the mean. Weighted that way, income tax and employee National Insurance
relieve \WageChannelTaxAndNI\ per cent of the diffuse loss against
\DispChannelTaxAndNI\ per cent of the concentrated one, a wedge of
\ScheduleWedgeWeighted\ points against a realised step of
\FactorialConcStepChannelSeeds.

Both of those figures come from the same simulation. They are the tax-and-NI
channels of one decomposition and the residual of the same decomposition, so
the \ScheduleWedgeOffsets-point difference between them is an accounting
identity, not a test the schedule could have failed. What it does is locate
the offsets: Universal Credit supplies most of them, because a complete loss
draws a means-tested award that a marginal cut never triggers, with National
Insurance above the upper earnings limit and the pension-contribution
residual accounting for the rest. Only the \ScheduleImpliedGap-point
single-worker wedge is computable outside the model, and it settles the sign
of the mechanism but not its size. A genuinely independent weighted benchmark
would apply the statutory schedule to the exposed earnings distribution
outside PolicyEngine; that distribution is not currently reported, which is a
gap in this draft. All figures in this comparison are on the
\FactorialChannelSeeds\ channel seeds; the \FactorialDraws-draw step is
\FactorialConcStep\ points, and comparing across the two bases would
overstate the offsets by more than a factor of two.

Three things prevent the simulation from being a restatement of that
arithmetic, and they are what the exercise contributes. First, the
income-tax wedge overshoots the realised step, because means-tested support
runs the other way: a complete loss draws Universal Credit that a marginal
cut does not, worth \FactorialConcStepUC\ points on that channel, and
National Insurance and the pension-contribution residual each
subtract a little more. Second, $m$ and $a$ are household objects here
rather than individual ones---partner earnings, rent, household composition
and the UC taper all enter---so the weighted aggregation over the exposed
population is not the representative-worker calculation. Third, the
counterfactual holds the \emph{aggregate} loss fixed, so the comparison
prices a redistribution of a given loss across workers rather than a change
in its size. The value added is quantification of a mechanism whose sign was
never in doubt, and identification of the offsets that determine its size.

One reading should be blocked before it forms. This is a
\emph{tax-progressivity} result, not a safety-net result. Universal Credit
moves \FactorialConcStepUC\ points of the concentration step against income
tax's \FactorialConcStepIncomeTax, and supplies only
\DispChannelBenefits\ per cent of the displaced gross loss in total. A
reader who takes the headline gap as evidence about the strength of the
means-tested safety net will have it backwards: the gap opens because the
\emph{tax} schedule relieves a marginal cut at the marginal rate and a total
loss at the average rate, and the means-tested response works against it.

One limitation of this split should be stated rather than left to the
artifact, and it is larger than a rounding remark. The channel decomposition
is computed on \FactorialChannelSeeds\ common seeds while the steps are
estimated over \FactorialDraws\ paired assignments. On those five seeds the
concentration step is \FactorialConcStepChannelSeeds\ points, against the
\FactorialConcStep\ points of the full design---a discrepancy of more than a
third---and the employment-state step is
\FactorialStateStepChannelSeeds\ points against \FactorialStateStep. Every
channel figure in this subsection therefore belongs to the five-seed run and
should be read as a decomposition of the \emph{mechanism}, not as an
accounting identity for the headline numbers. Where the paper quotes a
channel share and a headline rate in the same sentence, the seed basis is
stated. Re-running the split at the full draw count would dissolve the
problem and is inexpensive; it is listed among the outstanding items in
Section~\ref{sec:discussion}.

One limit of this design should be stated before the readings. With the
aggregate held fixed and concentrated on the ten records a displacement draw
selects, those records lose everything by construction, so the
``concentration and selection'' step confounds \emph{how concentrated} the
loss is with \emph{who} bears it; there is no free variation between them in
a three-cell design. A continuous sweep separates them: impose each
division's declared loss $s_j$ as a proportional cut of fraction
$\phi^{\text{eff}}_j=\max(\phi,s_j)$ on a randomly selected
$s_j/\phi^{\text{eff}}_j$ share of its exposed employees, so the expected
wage-bill loss is invariant by construction while its concentration varies
from fully diffuse to total. The clip matters: below its own $s_j$ a division
cannot concentrate further without under-delivering its aggregate, so it sits
at its diffuse endpoint until the grid reaches it. That sweep nests both endpoints of the
present design---at $\phi\le\min_j s_j$ it reproduces the diffuse wage cut
record for record, and at $\phi=1$ it reproduces the concentrated cell
exactly under Bernoulli assignment---and traces the curvature of the
effective schedule between them.
\path{analysis/concentration_sweep.py} implements it
(\texttt{make concentration-sweep}); it requires a licensed-data run and is
not reported here. It is the natural central object of this design and its
absence is the largest gap in the present draft.

Two readings follow, and the second constrains the first. The
concentration step is a genuine result about the UK statutory schedule:
within the modelled annual system, the diffuse-wage-cut versus displacement
gap is a \emph{loss-concentration} effect---complete individual losses
exhaust marginal-rate relief---and not an effect of unemployment status,
which Universal Credit's income-based means test barely registers. The
paper's earlier language of a ``statutory asymmetry between adjustment
margins'' was imprecise and has been replaced accordingly.

The employment-state step, by contrast, is close to mechanically zero and
should not be read as a finding about the United Kingdom. Every institution
in the UK system that conditions on employment status sits outside this
model: New Style JSA is not activated by the imposed transition, and monthly
assessment periods, the five-week wait, claiming frictions and work-search
conditionality carry no fiscal machinery here. The concentrated and displaced
cells zero the same earnings for the same workers, so the only remaining
difference between them is a status label that no \emph{statutory} formula reads;
income tax and National Insurance are identical by construction, and on the
\FactorialChannelSeeds\ channel seeds the entire step is a
\FactorialStateStepUC-point Universal Credit residual---those seeds giving a
step of \FactorialStateStepChannelSeeds\ points against the
\FactorialStateStep\ points of the full design.

That residual is not the employment-status label, and tracing it changes what
the step measures. Within policyengine-uk the \texttt{employment\_status}
variable is read by no tax or benefit formula at all: its only consumer is a
behavioural labour-supply module that this design switches off. What the two
cells actually differ in is \texttt{hours\_worked}, which the concentrated
cell leaves at baseline and displacement zeroes. Hours are load-bearing
through exactly one Universal Credit path---%
\texttt{hours\_worked} $\rightarrow$ \texttt{in\_work} $\rightarrow$
\texttt{uc\_childcare\_work\_condition} $\rightarrow$
\texttt{uc\_childcare\_element}---because \texttt{in\_work} is an
\emph{or} of positive hours and positive earnings. A displaced worker has
neither, so the childcare work condition fails and the childcare element
drops to zero; a concentrated-cut worker has zero earnings but positive
hours, so it is retained in full. Two further hours-sensitive gates lie
outside Universal Credit: the Housing Benefit worker disregard and the
council-tax-reduction non-dependant deduction.

The measured step is therefore an \emph{hours} effect operating on
childcare and two smaller in-work gates, not an employment-status effect. It
is small because childcare costs affect a minority of the exposed benefit
units, though the per-unit amounts are large. This sharpens rather than
weakens the reading below: the statutory system's response to job loss, as
implemented here, runs through hours-contingent entitlement rather than
through employment status, and the status label contributes exactly nothing.
Making the two cells differ in employment state alone would require zeroing
hours in both---an option the code now carries but which has not been run at
the licensed-data calibration, so the residual reported here remains an
hours effect. The magnitude of what is
missing dwarfs what is measured on either basis. New Style
JSA alone, at \pounds\JSAWeeklyRate\ a week for its 182-day maximum---taken
from \JSARateSource, with no 2026--27 uprating applied---is worth
up to \pounds\JSAMaxSpellAmount\ for a representative displaced worker on the
exposed-sector mean wage---about \JSACushionPoints\ percentage points of
cushioning, or between twenty-five and fifty-five times the employment-state
step this decomposition records depending on which seed basis is used, before any
allowance for conditionality or the timing of awards.
That comparison is an upper bound, since New Style JSA is taxable and is
deducted pound for pound from any Universal Credit award. Discounting it for
those two effects and for the contribution condition---\citet{figari2011}
find roughly three-quarters of the newly unemployed qualify in the United
Kingdom---leaves something of the order of two to three points. Against a
\PrimaryCushionDifference-point gap, a pipeline that simulated the single
most obvious status-contingent rule in the UK system would therefore report a
split nearer three-quarters to one-quarter than the one measured here. The
decomposition's \emph{sign} is robust; its division of the gap is not, and
this paper does not report one.

The honest summary is therefore narrower than the arithmetic invites. The
decomposition establishes that loss concentration, not employment status,
drives the gap \emph{inside a static annual statutory model}, and it bounds
what such a model can attribute to the extensive margin at all. It is not
evidence that job loss and an equivalent in-work earnings collapse are
fiscally alike in the United Kingdom, and the paper's contribution rests on
the concentration channel rather than on the state channel, which this design
cannot measure. Even that is a claim about the mechanism and not about its
size. The concentrated cell zeroes the drawn workers' earnings, so their
benefit units acquire the same Universal Credit entitlement as the displaced
ones---and therefore the same frozen claiming flag suppresses that
entitlement in both, while leaving the diffuse cell, whose households rarely
cross into new entitlement, almost untouched. Relaxing the claiming
convention would raise the concentrated and displaced cells together and so
\emph{shrink} the concentration step, by roughly three points on the channel
arithmetic. The step is identified in sign and mechanism; its magnitude is
convention-dependent by something between a third and two-thirds of itself.

\paragraph{Assignment-design and record-support audit.}\label{sec:results-assignment} At the unit 12-month
stress, balanced and Bernoulli integration agree closely: cushioning is
\BalancedUnitCushion\ versus \BernoulliUnitCushion\ per cent and Exchequer
cost is \pounds\BalancedUnitExchequer\ versus
\pounds\BernoulliUnitExchequer\ million. Balancing reduces gross-loss SD to
\BalancedGrossSDRatio\ of the Bernoulli value and Exchequer SD to
\BalancedExchequerSDRatio, but it cannot repair sparse survey support. The
unit displacement loss has only \UnitEffectiveLossRecords\ effective record
contributions and its largest record supplies \UnitMaxRecordLossShare\ per
cent on average. At the OBR three-month scale these deteriorate to
\OBRThreeMonthEffectiveLossRecords\ and
\OBRThreeMonthMaxRecordLossShare\ per cent. The smallest displacement rows
are consequently sensitivity evidence, not precise population estimates.
Balancing also has a cost that the aggregate agreement does not reveal:
conditioning on the wage-bill target changes record-level marginal inclusion
probabilities away from the declared $s_j$, and with the largest record
supplying \UnitMaxRecordLossShare\ per cent of the loss on average, a reader
is entitled to know whether that record is selected at its nominal rate. The
repository emits a per-record empirical inclusion-probability diagnostic
comparing the balanced and Bernoulli designs for exactly this purpose
(\path{analysis/assignment_inclusion_diagnostic.py}, \texttt{make
assignment-inclusion}); it requires a licensed-data run and is not yet
reported here, which is a gap in the present draft rather than a resolved
question.
Across leave-each-sector-out reruns for all \LeaveSectorCount\ exposed
divisions (\LeaveSectorDraws\ assignments each, a lighter design than the
primary table's \SubmissionDraws), the wage-minus-displacement cushioning difference remains positive,
ranging from \LeaveSectorCushionDifferenceMin\ to
\LeaveSectorCushionDifferenceMax\ percentage points. No single division
creates or reverses the primary contrast.

\paragraph{Record-resampling sensitivity.}\label{sec:results-bootstrap} Assignment SDs do not measure
sensitivity to which households the FRS sampled, so the primary contrast is
additionally scored with a household record-resampling exercise:
\BootstrapReplicates\ multinomial resamples of the
\BootstrapHouseholds\ households, applied to per-household contributions
from \BootstrapDraws\ common assignment draws of each unit 12-month margin,
so that resampling variation is measured around the draw-averaged statistic.
On that estimator the wage-minus-displacement cushioning difference is
\BootstrapCushionDifference\ percentage points with a resampling standard
error of \BootstrapCushionDifferenceSE\ points and a 2.5th--97.5th
percentile range of \BootstrapCushionDifferenceCILower\ to
\BootstrapCushionDifferenceCIUpper\ points. This is deliberately not
presented as a significance test: the two counterfactuals are imposed
scenarios, so their contrast is partly generated by construction, and the
resampling captures record composition only---not the FRS complex survey
design (no PSU or stratum identifiers exist in the research file), not
tariff-calibration, incidence, concordance or model-parameter uncertainty,
and not behavioural or claiming uncertainty, all of which remain scenario
dimensions. The range should be read as robustness of the contrast to
record composition, nothing stronger. Margin-level resampling SEs are
\BootstrapDisplacementCushionSE\ points (displacement cushioning),
\BootstrapWageCushionSE\ points (wage-cut cushioning), and
\pounds\BootstrapDisplacementExchequerSE\ million and
\pounds\BootstrapWageExchequerSE\ million on the respective Exchequer costs.
Two conventions should be noted, and they do not push the
same way. A resample can zero a displacement draw's
gross loss, leaving that draw's cushioning ratio undefined; replicates
average over the defined draws, and near-zero-gross resamples with unstable
ratios are retained, which \emph{widens} the interval and is what makes it
asymmetric about its point. The second convention narrows it: the
replicates reweight households while
holding the original displacement assignment fixed, so the interval is
conditional on which records those draws happened to select and is narrower
than a fully re-drawn bootstrap would be. Re-running the assignment inside
each replicate is computationally out of reach here. A ratio-of-pooled-sums
estimator, which is not vulnerable to the near-zero denominators, is
implemented in \path{analysis/bootstrap_uncertainty.py} but is absent from
the stored artifact, which predates it; \texttt{make bootstrap-summary}
re-emits it from the cached per-household contributions.

\paragraph{Leave-one-record-out.} Because the displacement side of the
contrast rests on few effective records, each of the
\LOOHouseholds\ FRS households that contributes a positive weighted gross
loss to any of the \LOODraws\ cached displacement draws is removed in
turn---from both margins, since the same survey record underlies both
counterfactuals---and the draw-averaged contrast is recomputed exactly from
stored per-household contributions. The contrast remains positive in every
case, ranging from \LOOMin\ to \LOOMax\ percentage points around the
\LOOPoint-point resampling-cache value; the most influential single record
moves it by \LOOMaxShift\ points. No individual FRS household creates the
headline gap, although the sparse support continues to rule out subgroup,
regional and constituency readings of the displacement scenarios.

\paragraph{Pension-contribution channel.}\label{sec:results-pension} The HBAI disposable-income concept treats employee pension and salary-sacrifice contributions as an outflow, so a hypothesis worth testing is that part of the headline gap is reduced retirement saving rather than tax--benefit insurance. It is not. Recomputing both margins under a pension-gross income concept (disposable income plus employee pension and salary-sacrifice contributions, unit 12-month stress, the primary balanced design over \PensionSeeds\ paired assignments) lowers wage-cut cushioning from \PensionWageCushionHBAI\ to \WageCushionPensionGross\ per cent and displacement cushioning from \PensionDisplacedCushionHBAI\ to \DisplacedCushionPensionGross\ per cent---the pension outflow falls by close to the same share of the gross loss on both margins (\PensionShareWage\ and \PensionShareDisplaced\ per cent respectively; wage cuts scale contributions, displacement zeroes them)---so the wage-minus-displacement gap moves only from \GapHBAIPension\ to \GapPensionGross\ percentage points. (As with the headline contrast, that gap is the mean of the per-draw differences rather than the difference of the rounded levels printed beside it; see the estimator-provenance note in Section~\ref{sec:results-cushioning}.) The headline contrast is a tax--benefit schedule effect, not a pension-accounting artefact. What the pension channel does affect is the \emph{level} of measured cushioning on both margins, and the implied loss of deferred retirement consumption is not captured by the cushioning rate.

\paragraph{Mixed and transition paths.}\label{sec:results-transition} The illustrative interior
calibration is the transition-equivalent path: 70 per cent of each sector's
declared loss is assigned to workers whose annual earnings and hours embody
a three-month re-employment lag before entering a services job at the FRS
28.3 per cent earnings penalty, with survivor cuts rescaled so the expected
sector loss remains common. It yields
$\FullTransitionCushion\pm\FullTransitionCushionSD$ per cent cushioning and
an Exchequer cost of \pounds
$\FullTransitionExchequer\pm\FullTransitionExchequerSD$ million---between
the bounds, as the composition logic implies. The surrounding response surface was computed over the declared grid
$\lambda\in\{0,.25,.5,.75,1\}$ but is not plotted here: the stored surface
predates the current production runs and its deterministic $\lambda=0$
endpoint reads about three points below the
\SubmissionUnitWageCushion\ per cent that Table~\ref{tab:cushioning} gives
for the identical scenario. A figure that needs a correction factor to be
read against the paper's own table should not be in the paper;
\texttt{make results} regenerates it. A churn-dominated 85/15
displacement--survivor split
($\FullRentSharingCushion\pm\FullRentSharingCushionSD$ per cent cushioning,
\pounds $\FullRentSharingExchequer\pm\FullRentSharingExchequerSD$ million)
lies between two of that grid's columns but is \emph{not} one of its cells:
it is a separate \ProductionDraws-draw mixed-margin run and should not be
read off the figure. The lower 14 per cent penalty sensitivity is reported in
the appendix.
The 70/30 share, lag and penalty are assumptions bounded by external
evidence, not estimated tariff responses.

\subsection{External magnitude and outturn checks}\label{sec:results-benchmarks}

The frozen HMRC customs build totals \pounds55.6 billion of 2024 US-bound goods exports before exclusions and maps \pounds52.5 billion to producing divisions. This is reasonably close to the ONS balance-of-payments headline of \pounds59.3 billion, given known conceptual and coverage differences \citep{onsus2024}. The reference full-schedule calibration predicts a \PredictedAggregateFall\ per cent aggregate fall across mapped divisions. The observed May 2025--February 2026 customs window falls \RealisedAggregateFall\ per cent against its two-year same-month baseline. This near equality is not a validation result: the reference calibration was not estimated from that window, and the outturn combines tariff effects with prices, exchange rates, anticipation, composition, rerouting and other demand shocks.

Sector agreement is much weaker. The stored validation artifact reports a rank correlation of \RankCorrTopTen\ between the full-schedule predicted and realised falls among the ten largest exposed divisions, and a correlation of \ShockRateCorrAll\ between full-schedule and observed downside shock rates across all mapped divisions. The rank correlation is degenerate rather than informative: the predictor takes only two distinct values across the ten divisions, and the two that carry the higher value have the highest and lowest realised falls respectively, so their mid-ranks cancel exactly. Ten divisions is in any case too small a set for either figure to be decisive; taken with the underpowered product-level split below, the fair summary is that the sector mapping receives no corroboration rather than that it has been refuted. Machinery and motor vehicles decline broadly in the expected direction, while aerospace, electrical equipment and basic metals do not. The aggregate match can therefore be coincidental and must not be used to validate Equation~\eqref{eq:shock}. As a separate policy benchmark, the OBR's March 2025 scenario uses a demand elasticity of 0.4, implying an 8 per cent export-demand fall after a 20 percentage-point tariff and a direct export-demand effect just under 0.2 per cent of GDP \citep{obr2025}. The paper's $\varepsilon=1$ reference is 2.5 times that elasticity, at the constant-wage-share benchmark $\pi=1$ (Section~\ref{sec:method-primitives}); the demand calibration, not the incidence assumption, is what makes it a strong stress rather than a central forecast.

\paragraph{Product--destination panel.} A monthly HMRC HS4
product--destination panel (Online Supplement, Sections~S1--S2) is the
strongest external test available of the sector calibration, and the
regenerated estimates do not support reading Equation~\eqref{eq:shock} as an
estimated first stage. The capped value-weighted specification, omitting a
\HMRCAnticipationSpec\ and computed \HMRCDofConvention, puts US-bound
exports \HMRCWeightedEffect\ per cent below the same products sent to
Canada, Japan and Australia. Almost none of that survives contact with the
zeros. The outcome fills absent flows with zeros under a $\log(1+V)$
transform, and when that is handled properly the estimate collapses: a
Poisson specification on levels gives \HMRCPPMLEffect\ per cent
($p=\HMRCPPMLPValue$, insignificant), and restricting to products trading
continuously in all four destinations gives \HMRCContinuousEffect\ per cent
($p=\HMRCContinuousPValue$). Both land near a third of the headline, so most
of the primary estimate rides on extensive-margin movement in the small
comparison destinations rather than on intensive-margin decline in the
United States. Equal product weights give \HMRCEqualEffect\ per cent
($p=\HMRCEqualPValue$), the differential pre-trend is now significant
($p=\HMRCTrendPValue$), and the estimate moves by more than twelve points
across sample-start choices. Placebo cutoffs in May 2019--23 remain
individually insignificant.

The tariff-intensity ordering cannot adjudicate any of this. Broad steel and
auto chapters fall \HMRCHighTariffEffect\ per cent
($p=\HMRCHighTariffPValue$) against \HMRCOtherEffect\ per cent for other
products, but the high-tariff group rests on fewer than eight effective
products and carries a borderline pre-trend of its own
($p=\HMRCHighTariffTrendPValue$): the comparison is underpowered rather than
decisive. The honest summary is that the panel shows a post-policy decline in
large US export products whose magnitude is not robust to how zeros are
treated, and which it cannot attribute to tariffs. That constrains the
bridge, not the estimand: the cushioning comparison conditions on a declared
loss identical across margins by construction, and the leave-one-division-out
audit (all \LeaveSectorCount\ divisions at \LeaveSectorDraws\ assignments,
contrast range \LeaveSectorCushionDifferenceMin--%
\LeaveSectorCushionDifferenceMax\ points) confirms that sector composition
does not create or reverse it.

\paragraph{Reconciliation with Dolls, Fuest and Peichl (2012).} The closest
published comparator reverses this paper's ordering, and the reversal is
informative rather than embarrassing. For the United Kingdom,
\citet{dolls2012} report an income-shock stabilisation coefficient of 0.352
(income tax 0.267, social insurance contributions 0.054, benefits 0.031) and
an unemployment-shock coefficient of 0.415 (tax 0.191, contributions 0.061,
out-of-work benefits 0.163): the unemployment shock is stabilised
\emph{more}. Here the displacement margin is cushioned \emph{less} than the
diffuse wage cut.

Decomposing the displacement margin on the same channels locates the
discrepancy, subject to one accounting caveat that must come first. The
channel split runs on \FactorialChannelSeeds\ common seeds while the
cushioning rates are estimated over \FactorialDraws\ paired assignments, and
on the stochastic margins the two do not coincide: the displacement channels
sum to \DispChannelSum\ per cent against the \SubmissionUnitDisplacedCushion\
per cent headline, a shortfall of \DispChannelShortfall\ points. The
deterministic wage-cut column sums to \WageChannelSum\ per cent, exactly its
own headline, which identifies the shortfall as seed-subset drift rather
than a missing channel. Dolls et al.'s coefficients, by contrast, add up
exactly. The comparison below is therefore between their decomposition and
this paper's \emph{five-seed} run, and the implied total difference is
\DispChannelSum--41.5 rather than the \SubmissionUnitDisplacedCushion--41.5
the headline would suggest. Re-running the channel split at the full draw
count would remove the caveat and is cheap; it has not been done here.

With that stated, the tax side agrees closely: income tax and employee
National Insurance together absorb \DispChannelTaxAndNI\ per cent of the
displaced gross loss here against 25.2 per cent in their unemployment cell.
The benefit side does not agree at all: all modelled benefits supply
\DispChannelBenefits\ per cent here against their 16.3 per cent, a factor of
roughly six. One further term has no counterpart in their decomposition and
should not be quietly absorbed: the \DispChannelResidual-point employee
pension and salary-sacrifice outflow, which is a reduction in deferred
saving rather than a public transfer and which is about a seventh of the
displacement channel sum, or two-fifths of the 11.2-point difference against
Dolls et al.---the same term that supplies about an eighth of
measured cushioning on the displacement margin (Section~\ref{sec:results-mechanism}). The decomposition is exactly the two terms above: moving from their income
shock to their unemployment shock, the tax component \emph{falls} from 0.267
to 0.191---the marginal-minus-average wedge---while the benefit component
rises from 0.031 to 0.163, the $B_{\text{out}}$ term. In their estimates the
transfer term wins; here it does not. The benefit channel is where the
reversal sits, and four features of the present design account for it, three of
them acknowledged limitations rather than findings. New Style JSA is not
activated by the imposed transition, removing the contributory component
that carries much of their benefit response. The claiming flag is frozen for
already-entitled non-claimants, zeroing UC for over half the displaced by
weight on the superseded $\varepsilon=2$ diagnostic, the only calibration for
which that share has been measured. The exposed population is unusually high-earning---a mean of
\pounds 48{,}272, well above the all-employee median---so means-tested
support replaces a small share of the loss even where it is paid. And their
coefficients are computed on the pre-Universal-Credit system of the late
2000s, with contributory Jobseeker's Allowance and tax credits rather than a
single tapered award.

The constructive reading is that this paper's displacement cushioning is a
lower bound \emph{with respect to the first two features}---the absent New
Style JSA and the frozen claiming flag---and the gap correspondingly an upper
bound with respect to them. It is not a bound overall: the unmodelled
capital test runs the other way (Section~\ref{sec:discussion}), and the
institutional change between the two systems has no established sign, so the
net direction is open. The first two features
are addressable within this framework and are the natural next runs; the third is a property of the exposed population and is a real
difference rather than a defect; the fourth is a genuine institutional change
between 2008 and 2026 and would move the comparison in an unknown direction.

\paragraph{Longitudinal worker benchmark (summary).} A five-quarter
longitudinal LFS benchmark (Study 9490) calibrates cell, earnings-band and
quantile-random-forest job-exit-risk models to BRES-adjusted manufacturing
targets and propagates each through the fiscal model while preserving every
division's expected wage-bill loss. Across all three LFS-shaped risk models,
mean displacement cushioning spans
\LFSSelectionCushionMin--\LFSSelectionCushionMax\ per cent against
\LFSUniformCushion\ per cent under uniform assignment. Two design points
must travel with those numbers. This family, and its uniform benchmark, use
the \LFSSelectionDraws-draw Bernoulli design rather than the
\SubmissionDraws-draw balanced design of the primary table, so the gap it
narrows is its own baseline of \LFSSelectionBaselineGap\ points rather than
the \PrimaryCushionDifference\ points reported in
Table~\ref{tab:cushioning}; and the three models agree
closely with each other---their spread is smaller than the assignment SD of
any single specification---but they do \emph{not} agree with uniform
assignment. Every LFS-shaped model raises
displacement cushioning, by
\LFSSelectionShiftMin--\LFSSelectionShiftMax\ percentage points, which
narrows the wage-minus-displacement gap from
\LFSSelectionBaselineGap\ points to
\LFSSelectionGapMin--\LFSSelectionGapMax\ points. Worker selection is
therefore not a second-order detail: it accounts for something between a
fifth and a third of the headline contrast, and it moves it in the direction
of a smaller gap. What this exercise shows is that the \emph{sign} and rough
scale of the contrast survive plausible re-shaping of within-division risk,
while its magnitude is sensitive to a selection model that the data cannot
identify. Person-level agreement between the three models is weak, and the
available LFS manufacturing target rests on \LFSManufacturingDonors\ donors,
so these are predictive baseline-transition benchmarks quantifying
selection-model sensitivity---not tariff-specific worker risk. Full designs,
distributions and tables are in the Online Supplement
(Sections~S3--S4).

One labelling point governs the rest of this subsection and the appendix reconciliation. The \ProductionDraws-draw legacy scenarios are run under the \emph{Bernoulli comparator}, not the balanced design of Table~\ref{tab:cushioning}. That is read from the scenario definitions in the source rather than from the artifacts, which predate the field that records it; the build now carries a guard that will check it once those families are re-run, and warns until then.
The consequence of the design difference is visible: that is why their gross-loss dispersion is an order of magnitude wider than the table's. Figures from the two designs are not interchangeable and are labelled at the point of use. The realised displacement output is consistent with the allocation design but too sparse for precise empirical incidence: the expected 9.7 selected FRS records per assignment produce \FullDisplacedWorkers{} thousand represented displaced workers and \pounds $\FullDisplacedGross\pm\FullDisplacedGrossSD$ million of gross loss. The gross-loss SD is nearly half its mean, and Exchequer-cost SD exceeds half its mean. The 100-assignment average is numerically more stable than an individual assignment, but it remains conditional on the observed 1,018 exposed records and the uniform within-division selection rule.

\paragraph{Take-up: an empty grid, and the margin that binds.}\label{sec:results-takeup} Because the
displacement-side cushion runs through UC, the claiming convention was
scored over \TakeupSeeds\ assignments at new-entitlement rates of 0.55, 0.80
and 1.00, with the stale pre-shock flag restored as a fourth convention.
Displacement cushioning is \TakeupDisplacedCentral\ per cent under all four,
and the maximum spread across them is \TakeupConventionSpread\ percentage
points. That result should not be read as robustness. The four conventions
agree not to within rounding but \emph{exactly}, to machine precision, across
a stochastic re-draw---which is only possible if no benefit unit is ever
re-drawn. That conclusion is \TakeupInertBasis. As
Section~\ref{sec:method-takeup} explains, almost every displaced
worker's benefit unit already holds a positive all-claim entitlement at
baseline through the in-work means test, so none satisfies the
zero-to-positive condition that the new-entitlement scope requires. The grid
varies a parameter with an empty domain, and the correct inference is that
this specification tests nothing, not that claiming does not matter. (The
level itself sits below the \SubmissionUnitDisplacedCushion\ per cent of
Table~\ref{tab:cushioning} because this grid runs on the Bernoulli comparator
over \TakeupSeeds\ seeds rather than the \SubmissionDraws-draw balanced
design; what matters here is the comparison across the four conventions, which
share it.)

What does matter is the convention the default scope freezes.
\TakeupZeroUCNonTakeupShare\ per cent of displaced workers, by weight, receive
no UC \emph{solely} because their benefit unit's stored claiming flag is
false, and that flag is a pre-shock draw calibrated as a population-wide
share rather than take-up among the entitled. That share, like the spread
below, comes from \TakeupEntitledSource---and from a single seed of it---%
because no diagnostic of this kind exists at the unit stress; both are quoted
for order of magnitude only. Re-drawing it for all entitled
changed units---the \texttt{all\_entitled} scope---moves displacement
cushioning from \TakeupEntitledStale\ per cent under the stale flag to
\TakeupEntitledFull\ per cent at full take-up, a spread of
\TakeupEntitledSpread\ percentage points---a convention grid, not an
estimate of claiming behaviour, and on the same source. Its
order of magnitude is nonetheless the relevant one. A spread of that size is
comparable to the entire \PrimaryCushionDifference-point wage-minus-%
displacement gap, so the headline contrast should be read as conditional on
a claiming rule that the survey data cannot pin down. The direction is
one-sided only with respect to claiming: allowing the already-entitled to
claim raises displacement cushioning and \emph{narrows} the gap, so the
reported figure is an upper bound in that respect. The unmodelled UC capital
test pushes the other way---redundancy payments against thresholds frozen
since 2006 would extinguish awards, lowering displacement cushioning and
\emph{widening} the gap---and the two effects are not commensurable with the
data in hand (Section~\ref{sec:discussion}). Re-running the \texttt{all\_entitled}
grid at the unit stress is among the outstanding items listed in
Section~\ref{sec:discussion}; the code and the \texttt{make takeup-entitled} target are in place and
require only a licensed-data run. Wage-cut results are take-up-insensitive
because few wage-cut households change UC entitlement at all.

\subsection{Mechanism}\label{sec:results-mechanism}

The regenerated decomposition and gate audit are five-assignment diagnostics,
but they clarify why the two margins produce different fiscal outcomes. A wage
cut keeps the worker inside the income-tax and National Insurance schedule, so
relief is concentrated in lower tax and contribution payments. Displacement
removes earnings altogether and shifts the response towards Universal Credit and
other means-tested support. The latter channel is nonlinear: entitlement
depends on household composition, existing income, rent, capital, and claiming
behaviour rather than on the individual earnings loss alone. One channel in
the decomposition deserves a name rather than a label. The ``other residual''
term---\DispChannelResidual\ per cent of the gross loss under displacement
and \WageChannelResidual\ per cent under the diffuse wage cut, so about an
eighth of measured cushioning on the displacement margin and nearer a
twelfth on the wage-cut margin---is overwhelmingly the
employee pension and salary-sacrifice outflow, which falls with earnings
under both margins and, under the HBAI disposable-income concept, offsets
part of the loss. That it is a fall in deferred consumption rather than
tax--benefit insurance is exactly the point tested in the pension-gross
recomputation above. The plain FRS
input contains no usable household-capital measure, so the model cannot
establish the empirical importance of UC's capital test. New-style JSA is not
activated by the imposed transition. The gate audit should therefore be read
as a decomposition of the implemented rules, not as a complete account of the
UK out-of-work system.

\subsection{Distributional incidence}\label{sec:results-margins}

Under full displacement, relative BHC poverty rises by
$\FullDisplacedPoverty\pm\FullDisplacedPovertySD$ percentage points. Under
wage cuts it is unchanged at displayed precision. The small national change
does not imply that exposed households are protected: the affected population
is limited, and many exposed workers begin above the poverty threshold. The national figures are the wrong object, and reporting them at all is a
concession to convention. What matters for welfare is the loss
\emph{conditional on being affected}: the same aggregate concentrated on ten
workers is far worse than spread over a thousand, and the system offsets less
of it, so a cushioning rate that falls as concentration rises understates the
insurance loss rather than measuring it. The repository computes the
conditional statistics---poverty among affected households at baseline and
post-shock, and the affected population---in \path{analysis/poverty_gap.py},
but only at an exploratory five draws whose standard deviation exceeds its
mean. \path{analysis/write_welfare_results.py} emits them as manuscript
macros and refuses to do so below \SubmissionDraws\ draws, so they are
withheld here rather than quoted at a precision the run cannot support
(\texttt{make poverty-gap-production}). This is the second substantial gap
in the present draft.

The national figures below are therefore distributional diagnostics rather
than population-wide forecasts, and the decile profile that earlier versions
plotted has been withdrawn: on a
typical stored draw four or five of the ten deciles record a
disposable-income change of exactly zero---the modal count is four on the
\SubmissionDraws-draw balanced design and five on the
\ProductionDraws-draw comparator---so the profile's shape is an artefact of
which handful of records a draw selected rather than a feature of the
distribution.


A constituency-level surface was produced in earlier drafts but is not
reported: it rests on imputed industry assignments rather than observed
local industry structure, and a map invites exactly the seat-ranking
reading it cannot support. The Online Supplement documents the
machinery for future use with observed local industry data.

\subsection{Fiscal incidence}\label{sec:results-fiscal}

Annual Exchequer costs are reported in Table~\ref{tab:cushioning}. The measure
is the aggregate simulated government-balance change and is broader than the
household cushioning identity. The cost combines lower income-tax and National
Insurance receipts with additional benefit payments. Employer National
Insurance contributions and the tax consequences of pension and
salary-sacrifice changes respond to the shock but sit outside employee
disposable income, which explains why the Exchequer cost does not equal the
household-side cushioning amount. Displacement also produces more assignment
dispersion because a small number of high-weight records can determine whether
earnings are lost entirely. Appendix~\ref{app:mc} provides the formal
reconciliation and draw distributions. Figure~\ref{fig:exchequer-results}
shows the assignment distribution.
