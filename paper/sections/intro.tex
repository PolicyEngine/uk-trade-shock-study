\section{Introduction}\label{sec:intro}

When the same aggregate labour-income loss can arrive as wage cuts or as
job losses, which does the UK tax--benefit system cushion better, and by
how much? This paper answers that question, with the 2025 US tariffs on UK
goods as the calibrated application. I hold a trade-exposure-calibrated
loss in employee earnings---roughly \pounds\FullWageGross\,million a year,
built from the 2025 US tariff schedule---fixed, and compare its fiscal and
distributional consequences when it arrives as incumbent wage reductions,
as displacement, or as mixtures of the two with re-employment penalties. The central finding
is a large statutory asymmetry. Defining the cushioning rate as
$1-\Delta Y/\Delta E$, where $\Delta E$ is the gross employee-earnings loss
and $\Delta Y$ the household disposable-income loss---the income
stabilisation coefficient of \citet{dolls2012}---proportional wage cuts
are cushioned at \SubmissionUnitWageCushion\ per cent while displacement is
cushioned at
$\SubmissionUnitDisplacedCushion \pm \SubmissionUnitDisplacedCushionSD$ per
cent---a gap of \PrimaryCushionDifference\ percentage points, with a
household-bootstrap 95 per cent interval of
\BootstrapCushionDifferenceCILower\ to
\BootstrapCushionDifferenceCIUpper\ points; the interval understates
sampling uncertainty because it omits geographic clustering and the first
stage. The mechanism
is institutional: an in-work earnings reduction is offset automatically at
marginal income-tax and National Insurance rates, whereas whole-job loss is
relieved nearer average rates and depends on Universal Credit, which is
gated by means tests, capital rules, and claiming behaviour---though the
poles differ in loss concentration as well as employment state, and
concentration does almost all of the work: the employment state itself
contributes \FactorialStateShare\ per cent of the gap
(Section~\ref{sec:results-mechanism}). That near-zero state step is a
property of the modelled rules, not of the UK out-of-work system in full:
New Style JSA---the one instrument that responds to the employment state
but not to an in-work earnings loss---is not simulated, and its omission is
bounded at up to \JSACushionPoints\ points of displacement cushioning, so
the state step would move against the headline gap once it is included. The gap is not
an artefact of the HBAI treatment of pension saving: recomputed under a
pension-gross income concept, both margins' cushioning falls by four to five
percentage points but the wage-minus-displacement contrast is essentially
unchanged (Section~\ref{sec:results-cushioning}).

The comparison matters because the UK tax--benefit system is nonlinear. For
an equal aggregate loss, a broad reduction in earnings leaves most households
in work and inside the tax schedule, whereas a concentrated loss moves
households across benefit-eligibility thresholds. Fiscal incidence therefore
depends on who loses earnings, whether the loss is temporary, and whether
employment resumes---and a single wage-cut or displacement scenario should
not be read as a forecast of realised trade adjustment. The two poles also
price a structural disagreement: flexible-wage models turn a tariff primitive
into a wage shock \citep{ignatenko2025}, while downward-nominal-rigidity
models turn the same primitive into a job shock \citep{rodriguezclare2025}.
Rather than endogenising the margin, I hold the aggregate loss fixed and
report what each pole implies for households and the Exchequer.

The epistemic status of every input is stated once, here, and in the
design table of Section~\ref{sec:method-primitives}, rather than repeated
throughout. The estimand is conditional: the statutory tax--benefit and
distributional response to a specified earnings-loss path, not a causal
estimate of the 2025 tariff episode. The 2025 US tariff schedule supplies
sector weights and shock magnitudes through a transparent accounting bridge
(tariff rate $\times$ US-export intensity $\times$ a demand calibration
$\times$ a wage-bill-incidence assumption); the bridge deliberately omits
prices, quantities, imported inputs, productivity, profits, and firm entry.
Reported $\pm$ values are assignment dispersion from the Monte Carlo
allocation of displacement, not standard errors; survey-sampling uncertainty
is scored separately by a household bootstrap. Mixed-path shares are
disciplined by external evidence but not estimated.

One negative result should be on the table from the outset. A monthly HMRC
product--destination panel shows a post-policy decline in US-bound exports
only in its capped value-weighted specification---concentrated in large
export products, absent under equal weights---and at sector rank the
calibration fails its own falsification
test: the rank correlation between predicted and realised sector export
falls among the ten largest exposed divisions is zero
(Section~\ref{sec:results-benchmarks}). This failure bounds the paper's
claims---the sector calibration must not be read as measured tariff
incidence---but it does not touch the cushioning comparison, which
conditions on a declared loss rather than on the bridge. The
leave-one-division-out audit across all \LeaveSectorCount\ exposed divisions
shows the wage-minus-displacement contrast surviving every rerun
(range \LeaveSectorCushionDifferenceMin--%
\LeaveSectorCushionDifferenceMax\ points): the calibration determines
\emph{who} is shocked, not the statutory asymmetry between intensive and
extensive relief.

The second stage uses the 2024--25 Family Resources Survey and PolicyEngine
UK to compute taxes, National Insurance, benefits, household disposable
income, poverty, inequality, and Exchequer effects. I report a common
aggregate loss under alternative adjustment paths, decompose the result by
tax and benefit channel, and show sensitivity to spell duration, worker
selection, benefit take-up, and re-employment. Between the polar bounds, an
illustrative interior transition path---70 per cent of the loss borne by
workers who re-enter services jobs at an FRS-calibrated earnings penalty
after a three-month lag, the rest by survivor wage cuts---yields
\FullTransitionCushion\ per cent cushioning and an Exchequer cost of
\pounds\FullTransitionExchequer\,million. One caveat travels with that
number: the lag is annualised into earnings and hours, so transition
workers remain employed for benefit purposes and no within-year
unemployment or benefit spell is simulated
(Section~\ref{sec:method-margins}). The response surface around the
bounds, not any single interior point, is the paper's central object.

The paper makes three contributions. First, it quantifies the statutory
offset structure behind the asymmetry, which no schedule arithmetic alone
delivers: the wage-bill-weighted tax-and-NI wedge between the margins is
\ScheduleWedgeWeighted\ points, pulled back towards the headline gap by
\ScheduleWedgeOffsets\ points of means-tested offsets---an accounting of
levels. A separate sensitivity shows the displacement-side cushion depends
on claiming behaviour: moving the new-entitlement take-up rate across its
grid shifts displacement cushioning by \TakeupConventionSpread\ points,
itself about half the gap. In doing so it
updates the UK cell of \citet{dolls2012} to the Universal Credit era and
reverses their ordering: the intensive margin is now the better-insured one.
Second, it shows why the incidence margin matters even when aggregate lost
earnings are identical, and that loss concentration, not employment state,
carries the asymmetry. Third, it provides a reproducible UK microsimulation
framework for mapping trade-exposure-calibrated earnings losses into fiscal
and distributional outcomes, with the assumptions that matter
most---timing, take-up, pensions, and worker selection---scored as named
sensitivities. The remainder of the paper describes
the evidence and scenario design, presents the polar bounds and mixed paths,
reports policy counterfactuals, and discusses limitations and
reproducibility.
