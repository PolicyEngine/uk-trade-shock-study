\section{Literature}\label{sec:literature}

The paper draws on four literatures:
the household incidence of trade policy, the microsimulation of shocks
through statutory tax--benefit systems, the distributional analysis of
price shocks and benefit indexation, and the UK's episode-specific
empirical record since 2020. Each has done part of what this paper does; none has joined them. A
note on terms: in trade economics ``trade shock'' usually denotes an
import-competition shock to local labour markets
\citep{autordornhanson2013}; this paper uses it more broadly, for
trade-related price and earnings shocks to households.

\subsection{Trade to households}

The framework for pushing trade shocks onto household microdata is
best developed for developing countries: the World Bank's Household
Impacts of Tariffs programme simulates tariff changes through both
expenditure and income portfolios for 54 countries
\citep{artucporto2021, artucporto2019}, and the OECD has published the
concordance machinery for mapping CGE trade-model output onto
household-budget-survey categories \citep{luu2020}. The structural
anchor for the consumption side is \citet{fajgelbaumkhandelwal2016}:
trade's gains flow disproportionately to lower-income households through
traded-goods budget shares.

Evidence from the 2018--19 US tariffs
\citep{amitireddingweinstein2019, fajgelbaumetal2020, cavalloetal2021}
finds near-complete pass-through to US import prices, so the cost of
a US tariff falls largely on US buyers and reaches an exporting
country such as the UK through export demand and earnings rather than
through its own consumer prices. The 2025 tariff war produced a fast-moving
US applied literature, the Budget Lab's rolling distributional scoring
of the enacted schedules and the Tax Policy Center's implementation of
tariffs inside a working tax microsimulation model
\citep{budgetlab2025, tpc2025}, and an academic treatment of
two-sided incidence on household expenditure microdata
\citep{fleckpradhan2026}. The CGE--microsimulation linkage tradition
supplies the architecture for putting a trade model upstairs and
microdata downstairs \citep{robilliardrobinson2005, peichl2008,
colombo2010, cali2019}; of that tradition, only \citet{peichl2008}
carries actual statutory tax rules in the micro layer.

With the partial exception of the Tax Policy Center's model, which
carries tariffs through the US tax code but not through transfers,
none of this work contains a tax--benefit system responding to
the shock. The World Bank and OECD frameworks stop at market incomes
and expenditure; the
CGE-linkage papers (Peichl's flat-tax application aside) use
reduced-form income equations.

To my knowledge no UK shock arriving under a \emph{trade}
label has been run through a statutory tax--benefit calculator (EUROMOD, UKMOD, TAXBEN or PolicyEngine); this paper does so for the tariff earnings scenario and computes the legislated response to the energy and TCA food-price episodes (Section~\ref{sec:results-secondstage}). The claim is
deliberately narrow: price and energy shocks as such have been run
through these models (the close antecedents are discussed in the next
subsection), and what is new is the trade shock as the input and the
episode-comparative design, not the machinery.

\subsection{Shocks through statutory rules}

That machinery exists and has been applied to income, employment and inflation shocks, but rarely to trade shocks. \citet{dolls2012} measure the stabilisation coefficients
of the US and European systems against stylised income and unemployment
shocks; \citet{brewertasseva2021} impose the observed Covid
labour-market shock on FRS microdata through UKMOD and decompose the
cushioning into automatic and discretionary parts; the EUROMOD
nowcasting tradition supplies the labour-transition machinery
\citep{navicke2014}.

Two studies come close enough to this paper's rulebook axis that the
claim must be stated narrowly. \citet{amores2025} run the 2022
inflation shock and its offsetting fiscal measures through EUROMOD for
the EU, separating income-support from price-containing instruments
(in effect a price-shock stabilisation coefficient, and the direct
antecedent of what this paper computes); they find fiscal measures
recovered roughly a third of welfare losses while targeting them
poorly.

\citet{popova2026} are closer still on the UK: UKMOD with LCFS
consumption imputed onto the FRS, disposable and consumable income, and
an explicit CPI-versus-earnings indexation counterfactual over
2019--2023. Neither isolates the September-CPI \emph{timing} lag or the
Energy Price Guarantee as a separate layer, or treats a trade shock as the input. That quartet, timing lag, discretionary energy layer, trade shocks as inputs, and the comparison across episodes, is what this paper
adds, and it is a narrower claim than ``nobody has measured price-shock
stabilisation.''

The conceptual gap it rests on is old and explicit: simulating a
supply shock in FRB/US, \citet{cohenfollette2000} found that
automatic stabilisers do very little against a supply shock, and only
modestly damp demand shocks, a result reported in a macro model rather than on microdata. \citet{paulus2020} supply the canonical treatment of
non-indexation as a distributional phenomenon, and
\citet{leventi2024} the nearest existing treatment of indexation
itself as a stabiliser.

For the earnings channel, two results motivate treating wage cuts
and displacement as bounds. \citet{ignatenko2025} and
\citet{rodriguezclare2025} model the same 2025 tariffs with flexible
and with rigid wages. And \citet{hummels2014} and
\citet{garinsilverio2024} find that export-demand shocks pass into
incumbent wages at elasticities well below one, so the wage-cut
margin is an upper bound on the wage response.

\subsection{Price shocks, indexation and the rulebook}

\citet{cribb2023} quantified the
2022--23 uprating shortfall directly (benefits rose 3.1 per cent
against roughly ten per cent inflation; recipients would not be made whole
until April 2025 on then-current forecasts) and scored the flat cost-of-living payments against
a timely-indexation counterfactual, finding the discretionary route
cost more while leaving hundreds of thousands of means-tested
households worse off than indexation would have. \citet{obrwelfare2022}
puts the aggregate real fall at around five per cent, some
\pounds 12 billion, in 2022--23.

\citet{judgemurphy2023} show that lags do not wash out over a
cycle: across fifteen years, working-age benefits were uprated below
outturn inflation in ten fiscal years. Internationally, \citet{eckerstorfer2025} microsimulate
Austria's inflation episode with lagged wage and pension adjustment.
This paper adds neither the discovery of the lag nor a better estimate
of it, only its pricing against \emph{trade-shock} incidence.

\begin{sloppypar}
\citet{jaravel2021} reviews evidence that trade affects price levels roughly
similarly across income groups, innovation rather than trade driving
inflation inequality. The episodes here are consistent with that as a
statement about trade \emph{in general} and inconsistent with it as a
statement about these shocks in particular: what makes the UK's
negative episodes regressive is not trade exposure as such but the
categories they landed on, food and domestic energy, whose budget
shares fall steeply with income (Figure~\ref{fig:budget-shares}).
Where a trade shock lands on clothing, as the UK--India agreement does,
this paper finds exactly the flat incidence Jaravel describes.

The method template for the expenditure channel predates all of this:
\citet{cravinolevchenko2017} showed after Mexico's 1994 devaluation
that lower-income households' cost of living rose about half again as much
as higher-income households', because they spend more on tradeables and buy
cheaper varieties within categories, the second mechanism being one
that category-level data cannot see. The
decomposition machinery for price shocks is also established:
\citet{sologon2025} decompose inflation's welfare and distributional
impact across expenditure patterns, and \citet{chenlevell2024} measure heterogeneous household price experiences
in scanner data. Their finding is a direct bound on this paper's
decile-table analysis: inflation inequality surged in 2021--23 partly because
cheaper goods inflated fastest, a within-category quality margin that
published expenditure-by-decile tables hold fixed and therefore
understate.

On targeting, \citet{curci2025} find for Italy that social bonuses
costing a fifth as much as untargeted VAT and excise cuts drove most of
the inequality reduction.
\end{sloppypar}

On the price-response side, \citet{levell2025} is the frontier and a
standing challenge to any decile-based exercise: using transaction
data they find income explains only a small share of the variation in
energy spending, so within-decile dispersion in exposure dwarfs the
between-decile gradient, and they show that an optimal package retains
a strictly positive price subsidy even when transfers can condition on
income and past usage.

\citet{bonnet2025} study the same choice for transport fuel in
France. \citet{auclert2023} add an externality a
domestic incidence exercise cannot see: national price subsidies
insulate one importer at the cost of world energy demand. Cross-country,
\citet{ari2022} and \citet{amaglobeli2026} document that most European
crisis support was untargeted and price-distorting, while
\citet{nao2024} records the fiscal counterpart in the UK: an
initial \pounds 139 billion estimate for the eight energy support
schemes against an estimated final cost of \pounds 44 billion, of which the Guarantee's
outturn was \pounds 24.0 billion.

\subsection{The UK episode record}

Each episode has its own literature estimating the shock, reviewed with the
imported numbers in Section~\ref{sec:episodes}: the Brexit/TCA price
and trade effects \citep{bakker2023, bakker2026, breinlich2022, freeman2022}, the
2022 terms-of-trade and energy-price shock and its policy response, the
2025 US tariff schedule \citep{obr2025}, and the UK--India agreement's
impact assessment \citep{dbtindia2026}. The Brexit episode has the richest distributional record, and two
precedents sit close to this paper. \citet{breinlich2016} asked
literally who would bear the pain, distributing projected Brexit price
changes across income groups and household types, and
\citet{clarke2017} did the same for an MFN tariff scenario, finding
the largest hits on unemployed, single-parent and pensioner
households. Both are ex-ante scenario exercises predating the actual
border; this paper's contribution relative to them is realised
episodes, a statutory household stage, and comparison across shocks.

\citet{davenportlevell2021} supply the earnings-side counterpart
(barrier exposure by occupation, sector and region), and
\citet{alabrese2026} and \citet{dhingra2026} the 2026 regional
accounting, the latter with new TCA tariff and non-tariff-measure
estimates in a spatial general-equilibrium model, finding the damage
runs through real wages and productivity rather than structural
change.

For the euro area, \citet{pallotti2023} find consumption losses from
the inflation shock roughly flat across the income distribution, with
the incidence falling instead along an age gradient.

The record also contains a puzzle. \citet{breinlich2022} find the depreciation episode's consumer cost
roughly \emph{flat} across the income distribution; \citet{bakker2026}
find the non-tariff-barrier episode sharply \emph{regressive}, the
bottom decile's cost-of-living increase around half again larger than
the top's. Same country, adjacent years, opposite incidence, because
the two shocks landed on different baskets; Section~\ref{sec:results} shows the
same mechanism across four episodes.

What remains absent
is the tax--benefit response: the Brexit price studies do not model it; the energy-crisis literature scores the discretionary response
but not the trade shock as such; the FTA impact assessments contain, in
the Regulatory Policy Committee's words, household analysis ``developed further for
future FTA IAs'' \citep{rpc2025}. The gap this paper fills is the
join: one statutory household stage run against each shock.
