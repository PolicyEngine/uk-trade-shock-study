\begin{abstract}
\noindent Britain's post-Covid decade delivered three trade-policy
shocks---the TCA border (2021), the US tariffs (2025), the India
agreement (2026)---plus the sharpest trade-transmitted terms-of-trade
shock since the 1970s (2022) and a near-zero benchmark (CPTPP). Each
episode enters as a declared first stage from published estimates or
government projections and is mapped onto households through prices
and earnings; the two largest are carried to household records, with
the statutory tax--benefit system run on \SsHouseholdsRecords\
enhanced-FRS households. Three results. First, the negative shocks
were regressive through prices and the projected positive gains are
proportional and negligible. Second, the statutory system's automatic
response to a price shock is structurally zero---every means-tested
award conditions on nominal income, which a price shock does not
touch---and the one automatic price-side channel, indexation, ran a
year late: a two-vintage rulebook run puts the withheld crisis-year
uprating at \pounds\SsVinLagCostBn\ billion. What cushioned the
energy shock was discretionary---the energy-specific instruments
returned \SsGridPaperEbssRatePct\ per cent of the counterfactual
shock (\SsGridPaperEbssDOne\ per cent of the bottom decile's loss
against \SsGridPaperEbssDTen\ of the top's), and
\SsDiscretionaryRatePct\ per cent including the general
cost-of-living payments---while the Brexit food shock was cushioned
by nothing: no discretionary instrument was legislated for it.
Third, \SsWithinPct\ per cent of the variance in energy exposure lies
within income deciles rather than between them
(\SsTcaSsWithinPct\ per cent for the food shock): the regressive
gradients are real, and they are a small part of who was exposed.
\end{abstract}
