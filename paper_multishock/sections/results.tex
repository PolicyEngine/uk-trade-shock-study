\section{First-pass results}\label{sec:results}

\subsection{The comparative anatomy}\label{sec:results-anatomy}

Table~\ref{tab:ms-anatomy} is the paper's central object: the five
episodes side by side under the common lens. Three regularities
organise everything that follows. First, \emph{the negative shocks were regressive and the positive episodes
were not progressive}: on the realised 2022--23 path the energy shock
cost the bottom decile \MsEnergyRealisedPctSpendDecOne\ per cent of its
spending against \MsEnergyRealisedPctSpendDecTen\ per cent at the top
(the never-realised gross counterfactual would have been
\MsEnergyGrossPctSpendDecOne\ against \MsEnergyGrossPctSpendDecTen), the TCA food shock \MsTcaWindowAvgPctSpendDecOne\ against
\MsTcaWindowAvgPctSpendDecTen\ per cent of spending on the
window-average path (\MsTcaPctSpendDecOne\ against
\MsTcaPctSpendDecTen\ at end-state), while the India agreement's consumer
gains are spread almost exactly proportionally
(\MsCetaBottomPctSpend\ versus \MsCetaTopPctSpend\ per cent of
spending) and are two orders of magnitude smaller. Second,
\emph{regressivity follows the affected category's budget-share
gradient}: food takes \MsTcaBudgetSharePctDecOne\ per cent of
bottom-decile spending against \MsTcaBudgetSharePctDecTen\ at the
top, energy \MsEnergyBudgetSharePctDecOne\ against
\MsEnergyBudgetSharePctDecTen, while clothing and footwear, where
the CETA gains land, are flat across the distribution: the
composition of a shock, not just its size, determines who bears it. Third, \emph{the cushioning came
from different parts of the state}: the one earnings-led episode was
cushioned automatically (\MsUSTariffDisplacementCushionPct--%
\MsUSTariffWageCutCushionPct\ per cent through the tax--benefit
system, from the companion study), while the price-led energy episode
was cushioned by discretion, the Energy Price Guarantee absorbed
\MsEnergyCushionShareFyPct\ per cent of the gross shock on the
financial-year basis (the point-to-point cap ratio is
\MsEnergyCushionSharePct\ per cent), and the
automatic price-side mechanism, benefit indexation, arrived a year
late (Section~\ref{sec:results-rulebook}).

\subsection{Episode detail}

\paragraph{TCA food prices.} The headline incidence uses the
window-average path implied by the published cumulative: the
\pounds\MsTcaPublishedCumulGbp-per-household estimate implies an
average effect of about \MsTcaImpliedAvgShare\ of the 8 per cent
end-state over the window, giving \pounds\MsTcaWindowAvgGbpDecOne\
(bottom decile) to \pounds\MsTcaWindowAvgGbpDecTen\ (top decile) a
year, \pounds\MsTcaWindowAvgAggBn\ billion a year in aggregate, regressive as a share of spending
(\MsTcaWindowAvgPctSpendDecOne\ versus
\MsTcaWindowAvgPctSpendDecTen\ per cent) because food takes
\MsTcaBudgetSharePctDecOne\ per cent of bottom-decile spending
against \MsTcaBudgetSharePctDecTen\ at the top. (The estimation window opens in December 2019, thirteen months before
the TCA took effect; the linear-ramp and window-average conventions
both absorb the pre-entry months as zeros.) This is an anchoring
choice, not a cross-check that happened to pass: the pipeline's own linear-ramp
cumulative overshoots the published figure by a factor of
\MsTcaCrossCheckRatio, and the window-average path is obtained by
rescaling to the published cumulative. The TCA row therefore carries no
information from the pipeline beyond the ONS food-share gradient, and
the level is the published estimate's. The end-state figures
(\pounds\MsTcaAnnGbpDecOne--\MsTcaAnnGbpDecTen\ a year;
\pounds\MsTcaAggBnPerYear\ billion) are the unrescaled variant: the pipeline's linear-ramp cross-check against the published
cumulative comes in at ratio \MsTcaCrossCheckRatio, showing the price
effect was back-loaded, so end-state overstates the lived burden. The
household stage would carry the published path if this episode were
extended to it (Section~\ref{sec:roadmap}).

\paragraph{The energy shock and the EPG.} What households actually
faced in 2022--23 was a mean capped bill of
\pounds\MsEnergyRealisedMeanCapFyTwentyTwo\ (\pounds 1{,}971 for the
first half-year, the Energy Price Guarantee's \pounds 2{,}500 for the
second) against \pounds\MsEnergyRealisedMeanCapFyTwentyOne\ the year
before: a realised rise of \MsEnergyRealisedRisePct\ per cent costing
the bottom decile \pounds\MsEnergyRealisedGbpDecOne\ a year,
\MsEnergyRealisedPctSpendDecOne\ per cent of its total spending against
\MsEnergyRealisedPctSpendDecTen\ per cent at the top, and
\pounds\MsEnergyRealisedAggBn\ billion in aggregate. The
\emph{counterfactual} shock, the October 2022 cap of
\pounds\MsEnergyCapPeak\ carried through the financial-year mean, giving
\pounds\SsCounterFyCap\ against the FY2021--22 mean of
\pounds\MsEnergyRealisedMeanCapFyTwentyOne, $+$\MsEnergyGrossFactorPct\ per cent, is the
figure the policy prevented, and would have cost the bottom decile
\pounds\MsEnergyGrossGbpDecOne\ a year, \MsEnergyGrossPctSpendDecOne\
per cent of its spending. Three labelled qualifications bound that number, and each is a
different kind of bound. First, \emph{welfare versus cash}: the
fixed-basket figure is the first-order welfare measure, correct to a
first approximation by the envelope theorem, but households cut
consumption, so cash outlays rose by less. Applying the observed
weather-adjusted reduction in domestic gas consumption of
\MsEnergyObservedDemandFallPct\ per cent over the gas year to mid-May
2023 \citep{desnz2024} gives a cash-outlay burden of
\MsEnergyCashOutlayScalePct\ per cent of the fixed-basket figure
(\pounds\MsEnergyCashOutlayGbpDecOne\ for the bottom decile;
\pounds\MsEnergyCashOutlayAggBn\ billion aggregate). The two answer
different questions, what households paid, and what they lost, and
the gap between them is itself the foregone consumption. Second, \emph{how much of this is trade}: the retail cap bundles
wholesale energy, which is traded, with network, policy and operating
costs, margin and VAT, which are domestic. Ofgem's published component
breakdown settles the split \citep{ofgem2022}. Wholesale rose from
\pounds\MsEnergyWholesaleBase\ to \pounds\MsEnergyWholesalePeak\ of the
typical bill, from \MsEnergyWholesaleShareCapBasePct\ to
\MsEnergyWholesaleShareCapPeakPct\ per cent of the cap, so
\MsEnergyWholesaleShareOfRisePct\ per cent of the cap's \emph{rise} is
traded energy and the remainder domestic cost. The trade-transmitted
component of the realised burden is therefore about
\pounds\MsEnergyRealisedTradeTransmittedAggBn\ billion
(\pounds\MsEnergyTradeTransmittedAggBn\ billion of the gross
counterfactual). One clarification matters for what this number means. Wholesale is not
the same as imported: the wholesale price is set on a world-linked
market, so UK-produced gas repriced too, and the resulting transfer
runs from British households to British producers and, through the
Energy Profits Levy, the Exchequer, not abroad. The wholesale share
therefore measures exposure to a world-market-linked price rather than
a transfer overseas, and the trade-transmitted figures above should be
read in that narrower sense. The comparison with
\citet{dhingrapage2023}'s finding that \emph{more than}
\MsEnergyImportedShareLowerBound\ per cent of the CPI rise was imported
is offered only as an order-of-magnitude check, since their statistic
concerns the aggregate price index rather than the wholesale component
of one regulated tariff. Third,
\emph{timing}: the annualised run-rate holds the October 2022 cap for
a full year, which no household experienced; the six-month window
figure is \pounds\MsEnergyCushionAggSixMonthsBn\ billion of EPG
absorption against the OBR's \pounds\MsEnergyObrEpgBn\ billion
costing, a context anchor, not a validation. Within those bounds the shape is
robust: the Energy Price Guarantee truncated the rise to
+\MsEnergyNetFactorPct\ per cent, absorbing
\pounds\MsEnergyCushionGbpDecOne\ a year for a bottom-decile
household, \MsEnergyCushionShareFyPct\ per cent of the gross shock on
the financial-year basis (\pounds\SsEpgBn\ of \pounds\SsCounterAggBn\
billion, Section~\ref{sec:results-secondstage}); the point-to-point
cap ratio, (3{,}549-2{,}500)/(3{,}549-1{,}277), is
\MsEnergyCushionSharePct\ per cent and is not used further.
Because the EPG capped the \emph{price}, its cushioning share is
constant across deciles by construction. Two qualifications follow
from the frontier evidence. First, decile means understate the
problem the instrument faced: \citet{levell2025} show that income
explains only a small share of the variation in household energy
spending, so exposure within a decile is far more dispersed than the
gradient between deciles, an argument for the household-record stage,
where that dispersion is observable, and against reading any
decile-mean cushioning share as a statement about individual
households. Second, a proportional instrument is not thereby a
mistaken one: \citet{levell2025} find that an optimal package retains
a strictly positive price subsidy even when transfers can condition on
income and past usage, precisely because income targets energy
exposure so poorly. The finding here is descriptive, the cap
cushioned proportionally, not a welfare verdict. Whether the overall
discretionary response was progressive cannot be judged from the EPG
alone: the Energy Bills Support Scheme's flat \pounds 400, the
means-tested cost-of-living payments and the council-tax rebate, jointly of the same order as the EPG and deliberately targeted, are
not modelled in this first pass. Section~\ref{sec:results-secondstage}
settles the question on household records: with the whole stack
modelled, the discretionary response was progressive, not proportional.

One comparator needs reconciling. \citet{rf2022} report energy budget
shares of roughly 11 per cent in the second decile against 6 per cent
in the ninth; the shares computed here are
\MsEnergyBudgetSharePctDecTwo\ and
\MsEnergyBudgetSharePctDecNine\ per cent. The gap is definitional,
not substantive: this paper measures electricity and gas only, as a
share of \emph{total spending}, on equivalised-income deciles of the
FYE2022 vintage, whereas the wider comparator includes other domestic
fuels and is expressed against income. Because the episode's incidence
is linear in this share, the difference scales the level of every
energy number reported here, another reason the household-record
stage, which computes the share directly rather than importing it,
supersedes this pass.

\paragraph{The US tariffs.} The UK consumer side is zero, no
retaliation occurred, so this episode's incidence is entirely
earnings-led: a gross loss of \pounds\MsUSTariffGrossShockMillions\
million a year cushioned by the automatic tax--benefit system at
\MsUSTariffDisplacementCushionPct\ per cent (displacement) to
\MsUSTariffWageCutCushionPct\ per cent (wage cuts), the companion
study's headline carried over as this episode's second stage.

\paragraph{The India gains, and the near-zero benchmark.} Of the \pounds\MsCetaTotalMillions\ million of final-goods duty
reductions, \pounds\MsCetaAllocatedMillions\ million falls in the
three categories with published spending detail (clothing, footwear,
food and beverages) and is allocated across deciles; the remainder is
unmodelled. At full pass-through the CETA duty cuts are worth \pounds\MsCetaGainGbpPtHundredDecOne\ a year
to a bottom-decile household and \pounds\MsCetaGainGbpPtHundredDecTen\
to a top-decile one, \MsCetaVerdict\ as a share of spending, though
larger in pounds at the top (the top decile gains about three times as much in
cash), and economically negligible at any pass-through dial
(\pounds\MsCetaGainGbpPtFiftyDecOne--\MsCetaGainGbpPtFiftyDecTen\ at
50 per cent). The CPTPP row is a near-zero benchmark, included as a
data-limitation case rather than a placebo test: its
\pounds\MsCptppGdpBn\ billion long-run GDP estimate divides to
\pounds\MsCptppNaiveGbpPerHousehold\ per household per year with no
defensible distributional structure, and the pipeline reports exactly
that and nothing more.

\subsection{The rulebook seed results}\label{sec:results-rulebook}

The vintage axis is seeded with two statutory facts. First, the
uprating lag: Universal Credit's standard allowance rose 3.1 per cent
in April 2022 (indexed to the previous September's inflation) while
prices rose at double digits. Against month-by-month contemporaneous
indexation, an upper-bound counterfactual no benefit system
operates, a single adult's standard allowance fell
\pounds\MsUcLagCostGbp\ short over 2022--23
(\MsUcLagCostPctAllowance\ per cent of the annual allowance); against
the policy-feasible alternative of a single April 2022 uprating at
contemporaneous annual CPI, \pounds\MsUcLagCostFlatAltGbp. Both
figures are for the single-adult standard allowance only, before
elements and caseload weighting; the range, not either point, is the
seed result. Neither the lag nor its cost is a discovery of this
paper: \citet{cribb2023} quantified the 2022--23 shortfall and scored
the discretionary cost-of-living payments against a timely-indexation
counterfactual, and \citet{obrwelfare2022} put the aggregate real fall
at roughly \pounds 12 billion, a figure the second stage's
\pounds\SsUpratingShortfallBn\ billion (Section~\ref{sec:results-secondstage})
does not reproduce: the bases and price counterfactuals differ, the two
are stated side by side rather than reconciled, and the reconciliation
is an explicit task of the extension (Section~\ref{sec:roadmap}). What is new here is the
join: the lag priced against trade-shock incidence, on the same
households, across episodes. The lag also does not wash out over the
cycle: \citet{judgemurphy2023} count ten of fifteen years in which
working-age benefits rose by less than outturn inflation. The shortfall
is lost to timing alone, at exactly the moment the terms-of-trade shock
peaked. Figure~\ref{fig:uprating-lag} shows the gap month by month. Second,
sequencing: the \pounds\MsUcUpliftRemovalGbp-a-year
Covid uplift was removed in October 2021, months before the energy
shock landed, so the lowest-income households entered the largest
price-led trade shock of the period from a rulebook trough. Together
with the EPG's absorption of \MsEnergyCushionShareFyPct\ per cent of the
gross price shock, the seed picture of the British response is:
\emph{automatic on the earnings margin, discretionary on the price
margin, and lagged precisely where the price shocks bit hardest}, with
the progressivity of the full discretionary stack an open question for
the microsimulation stage. The microsimulation
stage turns these representative-household facts into
population-weighted decompositions.

\section{The second stage: the energy episode on households}\label{sec:results-secondstage}

\subsection{Method}\label{sec:secondstage-method}

The largest episode is run through the statutory system on
\SsHouseholdsRecords\ enhanced-FRS household records in PolicyEngine UK
\SsPolicyEngineVersion\ (the 2023--24 enhanced Family Resources
Survey, 2023 vintage), in the tax--benefit microsimulation tradition
surveyed by \citet{figari2015}. Four conventions govern the numbers that follow
and each is a choice a reader could make differently.

\emph{One basis for both paths.} The counterfactual and realised energy
paths are both financial-year mean caps: the FY2022--23 counterfactual
is \pounds\SsCounterFyCap\ (the April and October 2022 caps, no
Guarantee), the realised path \pounds\SsRealisedFyCap\ (April 2022 cap
then the Guarantee), and the FY2021--22 baseline
\pounds\SsBaseFyCap. This matters: comparing a point-to-point cap ratio
with a financial-year mean, as is tempting, silently changes the
denominator of the cushioning rate.

\emph{Rebasing (assumption).} The enhanced FRS calibrates energy
spending to current-vintage price levels, so its level reflects today's
prices while the cross-household distribution reflects quantities. The
weighted mean is rebased to the FY2021--22 mean cap, a factor of
\SsRebaseFactor, preserving each household's relative position. Every
shock aggregate below is linear in this factor and should be read as
conditional on it; the within-decile results are invariant to it. The
cushioning \emph{rates} are not: the flat-cash instruments (the Bills
Support Scheme, the cost-of-living payments) do not scale with the
rebase while the shock denominator does, so the discretionary rate and
the decile cushioning shares depend on the factor. The dependence is
quantified in the two-axis grid of Table~\ref{tab:grid}.

\emph{Coverage.} The Energy Price Guarantee enters as the difference
between the two capped paths, the Energy Bills Support Scheme as its
flat \pounds 400 to every weighted household (so the modelled
\pounds\SsEbssBn\ billion exceeds the roughly \pounds 11 billion
scheme outturn by about 14 per cent: the \SsWeightExcessPct\ per cent
weight excess plus a small residual from non-domestic-supply
households in the weighted base), and the 2022--23
cost-of-living payments on simulated entitlement in the simulation
year, an approximation in both directions: simulated entitlement is
not receipt in the actual 2022 qualifying windows, the \pounds 150
disability payment is modelled per household rather than per
qualifying individual, and Housing-Benefit-only households are
included although they did not qualify for the \pounds 650. The
council-tax rebate and the Warm Home Discount are not modelled. The
discretionary total is therefore an approximation with omissions on
both sides, not a bound.

\emph{Rule year (assumption).} The simulation scores the episode on
the 2023 parameter system and 2023 incomes, the vintage of the
enhanced-FRS base, although the episode is FY2022--23. The
rulebook-vintage run below scores the same households under the 2022
parameter system as the crisis-year counterfactual; the
discretionary-stack results are insensitive to the rule year because
the instruments enter as declared cash amounts, and the
cost-of-living eligibility flags are nearly identical under the two
vintages (\SsVinColHouseholdsRuleTwentyTwoM\ against
\SsVinColHouseholdsRuleTwentyThreeM\ million flagged households).

\emph{Weights.} All weighted arithmetic is delegated to the
survey-weighting library that PolicyEngine itself returns results in;
no weight calculation is hand-rolled. The person
weights gross to \SsPersonsM\ million people, in line with the UK
population. PolicyEngine's household grossing weights total
\SsWeightedHouseholdsM\ million households against an ONS count of
\SsOnsHouseholdsM\ million, so aggregates carry an upward bias of about
\SsWeightExcessPct\ per cent; ratios are unaffected. Deciles are
equivalised on the HBAI before-housing-costs concept, and burdens are
expressed as shares of household expenditure rather than income,
because a ratio with income in the denominator is dominated by
very low incomes. One consequence must be flagged: the burden
\emph{numerator} is rebased to FY2021--22 cap levels while the
expenditure \emph{denominator} stays at the enhanced FRS's
current-vintage price level, and that base is larger than the ONS
FYE2022 tables the first pass divides by; second-stage shares of
spending are therefore roughly half their first-pass counterparts by
construction, and the two stages' shares are comparable in shape but
not in level. The variance decomposition and the within-decile
quantile ratios are unaffected: putting the denominator on the same
price base rescales every household's share by a common factor, which
changes neither the between/within split nor any ratio of quantiles. Aggregate pounds, which share a basis, reconcile to
within about five per cent.

\emph{Reconciliation with the first pass.} On the same
financial-year basis the first pass puts the counterfactual shock at
\pounds\MsEnergyGrossAggBn\ billion and the realised path at
\pounds\MsEnergyRealisedAggBn\ billion, against
\pounds\SsCounterAggBn\ and \pounds\SsRealisedAggBn\ billion here. The
residual gap of about five per cent is smaller than the
\SsWeightExcessPct\ per cent excess of PolicyEngine's grossing weights
over the ONS household count, so the household base explains at most
part of it: the imputed consumption distribution and the rebase
convention offset the remainder, and the decomposition of the gap is
not attempted here. Ratios are unaffected and levels should be read
with the unreconciled gap in mind.

\subsection{The automatic response, measured}

Every means-tested award in the UK conditions on nominal income, and a
price shock leaves nominal income untouched: the automatic response is
therefore zero by the structure of the statutory rules, not as an
empirical finding. The tax--benefit calculator is run twice, once on
baseline inputs and once with energy inputs shocked to the realised
path, and the change in every entitlement is
\pounds\SsAutomaticBn\ billion (\SsAutomaticRatePct\ per cent of the
shock); but because no modelled entitlement takes energy consumption as
an input, this re-run confirms that the model encodes the statutory
structure rather than measuring an independent quantity, and the claim
rests on the rulebook, which is verifiable line by line. It is also a
first-order claim: a shock of this size moved nominal incomes in
general equilibrium, wage bargaining, self-employment margins, and
those excluded channels (Section~\ref{sec:framework}) would trigger
means-tested responses the first-order calculation cannot. This is
\citet{cohenfollette2000}'s macro finding, that automatic stabilisers
do little against a supply shock, at the household level, and sharper
than their version, because for a pure price shock the mechanical
response is not merely small but absent.

Two qualifications keep the claim honest. It is a statement about the
\emph{modelled} statutory system, so channels outside it, the Warm
Home Discount, capital drawdown altering means-tested entitlement,
passported eligibility shifting at the margin, self-employed income
responses, could move the true figure off zero, though all are small
relative to the shock. And it is an in-year statement: indexation is
the system's automatic price-side channel, and it arrives late rather
than never.

\subsection{Indexation arrives late}

Benefits rose 3.1 per cent in April 2022, indexed to the previous
September, against inflation over the year of about 10 per cent, the
benefit-erosion mechanism whose cross-national treatment is
\citet{paulus2020}. The
cost of that gap is here computed with a genuine rulebook-vintage
run rather than a flat base-times-gap multiplication: the enhanced
FRS is a single-period dataset, so every input column is copied to
the 2022 period and the \emph{same} households are scored under the
2022 and 2023 parameter systems. The legislated April 2023 uprating
(10.1 per cent) moves the fourteen uprated cash benefits from
\pounds\SsVinBenefitBaseRuleTwentyTwo\ to
\pounds\SsVinBenefitBaseRuleTwentyThree\ billion,%
\pounds\SsVinUpratingEffectBn\ billion through the full statutory
structure, tapers included, or \pounds\SsVinPerPointBn\ billion per
uprating point. At that measured rate, the \SsUpratingGapPp\ points
withheld in the crisis year cost
\pounds\SsVinLagCostBn\ billion (the final step is linear in the
rate, an approximation declared as such), against the roughly
\pounds 12 billion aggregate real fall of \citet{obrwelfare2022} on a
different base and counterfactual, and below the
\pounds\SsUpratingShortfallBn\ billion a flat multiplication of the
2023-rules base (\pounds\SsBenefitIncomeBn\ billion) would give,
because tapers claw back part of any uprating.
It is deliberately \emph{not} expressed as a share of the energy
shock: the numerator is the erosion of benefit income by all inflation,
and dividing it by one commodity's counterfactual would attribute to
energy what food, services and rents also did. What the number
establishes is narrower and still worth having, that the one automatic
channel available to a price shock was running backwards while the
shock landed.

\subsection{The discretionary response was large and progressive}

Against a counterfactual shock of \pounds\SsCounterAggBn\ billion
(\SsCounterRisePct\ per cent on the FY basis), the
\emph{energy-specific} instruments, the Guarantee
(\pounds\SsEpgBn\ billion) and the Bills Support Scheme
(\pounds\SsEbssBn\ billion), returned \SsGridPaperEbssRatePct\ per
cent (\SsGridPaperEbssDOne\ per cent at the bottom decile,
\SsGridPaperEbssDTen\ at the top). Adding the cost-of-living payments
(\pounds\SsColBn\ billion) takes the full stack to
\pounds\SsDiscretionaryBn\ billion, or \SsDiscretionaryRatePct\ per
cent. The attribution choice must be stated: the cost-of-living
payments were legislated against the general inflation episode, not
energy specifically, so dividing them by the energy-only
counterfactual over-attributes to this shock by the same logic on
which the preceding subsection refuses to divide the indexation
shortfall by the energy shock; both rates are therefore carried, the
energy-specific one as the conservative reading. On either attribution it was progressive, on the full stack,
\SsCushionPctDecOne\ per cent of the bottom decile's counterfactual
loss returned against \SsCushionPctDecTen\ per cent of the top
decile's, because flat payments are worth more relative to a small
bill and the targeted payments went where they were aimed. The
over-compensation of the bottom decile exists only on the full-stack
attribution: energy-specific, the bottom decile recovers
\SsGridPaperEbssDOne\ per cent. Two features
of the profile deserve stating rather than smoothing. The bottom decile
is \emph{over}-compensated on this accounting, at
\SsCushionPctDecOne\ per cent, as is the third at
\SsCushionPctDecThree; and the gradient is not monotone, the sixth
decile (\SsCushionPctDecSix\ per cent) sitting below the fifth. The
over-compensation is narrow in two ways: the bootstrap below puts it
within sampling error of exact compensation, and it says only that the
instruments returned about as much as the counterfactual energy cost
to those households, not that they were made whole against inflation
generally, which the preceding subsection shows they were not. The
first-pass inference that discretion cushioned \emph{proportionally}
was an artefact of modelling the price cap alone; adding the
instruments the cap sat alongside reverses it, and the correction runs
in the government's favour. Figure~\ref{fig:decomp} shows the anatomy in
pounds: the counterfactual shock by decile, split into the part the
household bore and what each instrument returned. A household bootstrap
(\SsBootReps\ resamples of the record base, following the practice
urged for microsimulation results by \citet{goedeme2013}) puts a 95 per cent
sampling interval of \SsRateLo--\SsRateHi\ per cent around the
\SsDiscretionaryRatePct\ per cent aggregate rate, and
\SsCushionDecOneLo--\SsCushionDecOneHi\ per cent around the bottom
decile's \SsCushionPctDecOne: the over-compensation of the bottom
decile is therefore not statistically distinguishable from exact
compensation, and the net borne amount spans zero in each of the
bottom five deciles (95 per cent intervals
\pounds\SsNetLoDecOne--\SsNetHiDecOne,
\pounds\SsNetLoDecTwo--\SsNetHiDecTwo,
\pounds\SsNetLoDecThree--\SsNetHiDecThree,
\pounds\SsNetLoDecFour--\SsNetHiDecFour\ and
\pounds\SsNetLoDecFive--\SsNetHiDecFive\ a year for deciles one to
five). These
are sampling intervals over the survey base only; imputation
uncertainty from the consumption donor models is not captured
(Section~\ref{sec:results-withindec}).

\subsection{The two-axis sensitivity grid}\label{sec:results-grid}

Table~\ref{tab:grid} varies the two conventions the headline rates
depend on: the rebase factor (the paper's computed \SsRebaseFactor,
an intermediate 0.8, and 1.0, no rebase) and the discretionary stack,
built up one instrument at a time. Two facts organise the twelve
cells. First, the Guarantee's cushioning rate
(\SsGridPaperEpgRatePct\ per cent) is invariant to \emph{both}
axes, to the rebase because a price cap scales with the bill it
truncates, and across deciles by the accounting identity already
noted, so every pound of progressivity and every point of
rebase-sensitivity in the headline comes from the flat-cash
instruments. Second, the headline is bounded, not fragile: the full
stack cushions \SsGridPaperFullRatePct\ per cent of the
counterfactual shock at the paper's rebase and
\SsGridUnityFullRatePct\ per cent with no rebase at all, and the
progressive gradient survives every cell: without rebasing, the full
modelled stack returns \SsGridUnityFullDOne\ per cent of the bottom
decile's loss against \SsGridUnityFullDTen\ per cent of the top
decile's. The qualitative
findings, discretionary, large, progressive, hold across the grid;
the bottom decile's over-compensation is the one result that is
basis-dependent, present at the paper's rebase
(\SsGridPaperFullDOne\ per cent) and absent without rebasing
(\SsGridUnityFullDOne\ per cent). One external anchor bears on the
choice of cell and must be confronted rather than footnoted: at a
rebase of one the EPG absorption is \pounds 27.2 billion, close to
the OBR's \pounds 27 billion EPG costing, while the paper's rebase
gives \pounds\SsEpgBn\ billion. The near-match is partly
coincidental, the OBR priced the Guarantee against the higher caps
then expected for 2023, over a longer window, on price-dependent
volumes, but it cautions against reading the rebased cell as the
truth and the unrebased one as a variant. The paper's headline
statements are therefore confined to what holds across the grid, and
the basis-dependent over-compensation is reported as exactly that.
One dial the grid does not vary is the counterfactual \emph{path}
itself: holding the October 2022 cap through a full financial year is
a convention, not a forecast, the Guarantee pre-empted that cap
before it ever bound, and an ex-ante articulated path would give a
different denominator. The choice is declared
(Section~\ref{sec:secondstage-method}) and varying it is extension
work.

\subsection{Exposure varies within deciles, not between them}\label{sec:results-withindec}

Figure~\ref{fig:paths} plots the three shock paths of the design on
one distribution: the gross counterfactual, the realised path, and the
cash-outlay path (the gross counterfactual scaled by the observed
demand response, the same convention as the first pass). The paths differ in level, not shape: policy and
demand response scaled the shock down without changing who it fell on.
The realised burden runs from \SsBurdenPctSpendDecOne\ per cent of
household expenditure in the bottom decile to
\SsBurdenPctSpendDecTen\ per cent in the top. The gradient is
regressive and not monotone, the third decile
(\SsBurdenPctSpendDecThree\ per cent) sits marginally above the
first. The share \emph{levels} are about half their first-pass
counterparts by the denominator convention declared in
Section~\ref{sec:secondstage-method}, so only the shape, not the
level, is comparable across the two stages; within that comparison,
energy exposure separates households far less cleanly by income than
decile tables imply.
Decomposing the variance of that burden puts
\SsBetweenPct\ per cent of it between deciles and
\SsWithinPct\ per cent within them on the paper's preferred basis
(expenditure share, trimmed at the first and ninety-ninth percentiles;
household-bootstrap 95 per cent interval
\SsWithinLo--\SsWithinHi\ per cent for the within-share);
untrimmed the between-share is \SsBetweenPctUntrimmed\ per cent and in
logs \SsBetweenPctLog\ per cent. The trimmed expenditure-share figure is the paper's estimate; the
near-zero untrimmed between-share is dominated by a handful of extreme
imputed shares in the tails and is reported as a data-quality
diagnostic on the imputation, not as a robustness check. On every
basis, though, the gradient the first pass measured is a small part of
the dispersion in who was exposed. Within the bottom decile alone the burden runs from
\SsPTenDecOne\ per cent of spending at the tenth percentile to
\SsPNinetyDecOne\ per cent at the ninetieth.

Two limits bound what this establishes. The exposure is
\emph{imputed}, enhanced-FRS energy spending is a quantile-regression
forest fitted to LCFS donors, so part of the within-decile dispersion
is donor-model variation, and the decomposition is a bound rather than
a measurement of true dispersion, the bootstrap interval above is
sampling uncertainty only, blind to the donor-model error, and a
direct validation of the imputed within-decile spread against the
LCFS donor sample, which observes it, is left to the extension; \citet{levell2025} use transaction
data precisely for this reason. And the estimand is the variance of one
commodity's expenditure share, not the variance of welfare changes from
product-level trade exposure, which is what
\citet{borusyakjaravel2024} decompose: the results point the same way,
but they are not the same quantity.

\subsection{A second episode through the second stage: the TCA food shock}\label{sec:results-tca-ss}

The common cushioning estimand is computed for a second episode, and
this one is a trade-\emph{policy} act. The TCA food-price first stage
(the window-average path of Section~\ref{sec:results}) is priced
against each household's imputed food consumption on the same
\SsHouseholdsRecords\ records, rebased so the weighted mean matches
the first pass's ONS FYE2022 food-spend base (factor
\SsTcaSsFoodRebase; the same convention as the energy episode's cap
rebase, with the same hybrid share denominators). The aggregate,
\pounds\SsTcaSsAggBn\ billion a year, therefore matches the first
pass by construction of the mean, the new content of this stage is
the distribution, not the level.
The burden is regressive (\SsTcaSsPctSpendDecOne\ per cent of
bottom-decile spending against \SsTcaSsPctSpendDecTen\ at the top,
on the second stage's share basis) and larger in pounds at the top
(\pounds\SsTcaSsGbpDecOne\ at the bottom against
\pounds\SsTcaSsGbpDecTen\ at the top), and \SsTcaSsWithinPct\ per cent of the variance in exposure
lies within deciles (trimmed basis; within the bottom decile the
burden runs \SsTcaSsPTenDecOne--\SsTcaSsPNinetyDecOne\ per cent of
spending at the tenth to ninetieth percentile). The cushioning
decomposition is short and is the point: the automatic response is
structurally zero, as for any price shock; indexation ran late, as
above; and no episode-specific instrument exists, nothing was
legislated against the TCA food-price effect. The contrast with the
energy episode must be drawn on the episode-specific basis, by the
same attribution logic as the discretionary subsection above: the
general cost-of-living payments that are the non-conservative part of
the energy stack also cushioned food-price inflation in 2022--23, so
they cannot count toward energy and as zero for food. Episode-specific,
the contrast stands and is the paper's sharpest comparative fact:
against one price-led trade shock the state legislated instruments
returning \SsGridPaperEbssRatePct\ per cent of the counterfactual
loss; against the other, of the same sign and the same regressivity,
it legislated none. Composition and salience, not incidence, decided
which shock got its own instruments.

\subsection{From incidence to demand and welfare}\label{sec:results-mpc}

The statutory incidence measured above is an accounting object. This
subsection aggregates it into two economic ones, keeping the paper's
conventions: every behavioural parameter is imported from the
literature and declared, nothing is estimated, and the outputs are
first-round policy-simulation quantities, no general-equilibrium
feedback, no intertemporal response.

\emph{Demand.} Each flow of the energy episode, the counterfactual
burden and the three instruments, is weighted by a marginal
propensity to consume assigned by equivalised-income decile, under
three declared scenarios: a gradient falling from 0.8 in the bottom
decile to 0.3 in the top, the income and liquidity pattern of
\citet{jappelli2014} and \citet{fagereng2021}; a flat 0.5, which
switches the heterogeneity channel off; and an asymmetric UK variant
after \citet{bunn2018}, who find MPCs out of income \emph{losses}
(about 0.5) far larger than out of gains (about 0.14).
Table~\ref{tab:mpc} reports the results. Three facts follow. First,
the counterfactual shock would have destroyed
\pounds\SsMpcBurdenGradientBn\ billion of first-round consumption;
with the modelled stack in place the net effect is
\pounds\SsMpcNetGradientBn\ billion, the support protected most of
the demand the shock threatened. Second, the instruments rank by
targeting: the cost-of-living payments protected
\SsMpcColPerPound\ of consumption per pound of fiscal cost, the flat
Bills Support Scheme \SsMpcEbssPerPound, the proportional Guarantee
\SsMpcEpgPerPound, the demand-efficiency ordering that MPC
heterogeneity predicts. Third, the asymmetric scenario is the
sobering one: if households cut spending out of losses at 0.5 but
spend transfers at only 0.14, the net first-round demand effect is
\pounds\SsMpcNetAsymBn\ billion, the stack protected incomes, but
much less of demand. The same logic gives the uprating lag its
demand-side reading: the \pounds\SsVinLagCostBn\ billion withheld
by late indexation was withheld from benefit recipients, the
highest-MPC households in the economy, so the demand drag per pound
withheld was at its maximum.

\emph{Welfare.} Weighting the counterfactual burden by
\citet{atkinson1970} social-welfare weights on equivalised income,%
the simplest member of the generalised welfare-weight framework of
\citet{saezstantcheva2016}, (proportional to $y^{-\gamma}$, normalised to mean one) gives a
regressivity premium of \SsWelfPremiumGammaOne\ at $\gamma=1$ and
\SsWelfPremiumGammaTwo\ at $\gamma=2$. The $\gamma=1$ figure is
deliberately reported although it is unflattering: the shock is
regressive in \emph{shares} but larger in \emph{pounds} at the top, so at log
utility the pound-concentration at the top roughly offsets the
share-concentration at the bottom, and only at higher inequality
aversion does the welfare cost exceed that of an equal-pounds shock.
Distributional statements about this episode therefore depend on the
inequality aversion the reader brings, and the paper reports the
mapping rather than choosing for them.
