\section{Decile-table results}\label{sec:results}

\subsection{The comparative anatomy}\label{sec:results-anatomy}

Table~\ref{tab:ms-anatomy} places the four episodes side by side
under the common lens, and Figure~\ref{fig:incidence-profile} plots
the three price profiles it summarises. First, \emph{both negative price shocks were regressive and the one positive episode was proportional}: on the realised 2022--23 path the energy shock
cost the bottom decile \MsEnergyRealisedPctSpendDecOne\ per cent of its
spending against \MsEnergyRealisedPctSpendDecTen\ per cent at the top
(the never-realised gross counterfactual would have been
\MsEnergyGrossPctSpendDecOne\ against \MsEnergyGrossPctSpendDecTen), the TCA food shock \MsTcaWindowAvgPctSpendDecOne\ against
\MsTcaWindowAvgPctSpendDecTen\ per cent of spending on the
window-average path (\MsTcaPctSpendDecOne\ against
\MsTcaPctSpendDecTen\ at end-state), while the India agreement's consumer
gains are spread almost exactly proportionally (under 0.02 per cent
of spending in every decile) and are two orders of magnitude smaller
than the energy shock and one order smaller than the food shock. Second,
\emph{regressivity follows the affected category's budget-share
gradient}: food takes \MsTcaBudgetSharePctDecOne\ per cent of
bottom-decile spending against \MsTcaBudgetSharePctDecTen\ at the
top, energy \MsEnergyBudgetSharePctDecOne\ against
\MsEnergyBudgetSharePctDecTen, while clothing and footwear, where
the India CETA gains land, are flat across the distribution: the
composition of a shock, not just its size, determines who bears it. Third, \emph{the modelled responses run
through different mechanisms}: the one earnings-led episode, a declared scenario, is cushioned
through the automatic tax--benefit system
(\MsUSTariffDisplacementCushionPct--%
\MsUSTariffWageCutCushionPct\ per cent), while the price-led energy
episode was cushioned by discretionary instruments: the Energy Price Guarantee absorbed
\MsEnergyCushionShareFyPct\ per cent of the gross shock on the
financial-year basis (the point-to-point cap ratio is
\MsEnergyCushionSharePct\ per cent), and the
automatic price-side mechanism, benefit indexation, arrived a year
late (Section~\ref{sec:results-indexation}).

\subsection{Episode detail}

\paragraph{TCA food prices.} The headline incidence uses the
window-average path implied by the published cumulative: the
\pounds\MsTcaPublishedCumulGbp-per-household estimate implies an
average effect of about 30 per cent of the 8 per cent end-state over
the window, giving \pounds\MsTcaWindowAvgGbpDecOne\
(bottom decile) to \pounds\MsTcaWindowAvgGbpDecTen\ (top decile) a
year, \pounds\MsTcaWindowAvgAggBn\ billion a year in aggregate, regressive as a share of spending
(\MsTcaWindowAvgPctSpendDecOne\ versus
\MsTcaWindowAvgPctSpendDecTen\ per cent) because food takes
\MsTcaBudgetSharePctDecOne\ per cent of bottom-decile spending
against \MsTcaBudgetSharePctDecTen\ at the top. This is an anchoring
choice: a linear-ramp cumulative overshoots the published figure by a factor of
\MsTcaCrossCheckRatio, and the window-average path is obtained by
rescaling to the published cumulative. The level of the TCA row is therefore the published estimate's; this paper adds only its distribution across deciles. The end-state figures
(\pounds\MsTcaAnnGbpDecOne--\MsTcaAnnGbpDecTen\ a year;
\pounds\MsTcaAggBnPerYear\ billion) are the unrescaled variant; the
\MsTcaCrossCheckRatio\ overshoot shows the price effect was back-loaded, so the end-state
rate overstates the burden over the estimation window. It is,
however, the continuing annual rate once the price effect is complete:
on that basis the food shock costs \pounds\MsTcaAggBnPerYear\ billion a
year, \MsTcaPctSpendDecOne\ per cent of bottom-decile spending against
\MsTcaPctSpendDecTen\ at the top. (The estimation window opens
in December 2019, thirteen months before the TCA took effect; the
conventions absorb the pre-entry months as zeros.)

\paragraph{The energy shock and the EPG.} What households actually
faced in 2022--23 was a mean capped bill of
\pounds\MsEnergyRealisedMeanCapFyTwentyTwo\ (\pounds 1{,}971 for the
first half-year, the Energy Price Guarantee's \pounds 2{,}500 for the
second) against \pounds\MsEnergyRealisedMeanCapFyTwentyOne\ the year
before: a realised rise of \MsEnergyRealisedRisePct\ per cent costing
the bottom decile \pounds\MsEnergyRealisedGbpDecOne\ a year,
\MsEnergyRealisedPctSpendDecOne\ per cent of its total spending against
\MsEnergyRealisedPctSpendDecTen\ per cent at the top, and
\pounds\MsEnergyRealisedAggBn\ billion in aggregate. The
\emph{counterfactual} shock, the October 2022 cap of
\pounds\MsEnergyCapPeak\ carried through the financial-year mean, giving
\pounds\SsCounterFyCap\ against the FY2021--22 mean of
\pounds\MsEnergyRealisedMeanCapFyTwentyOne, $+$\MsEnergyGrossFactorPct\ per cent, is the
figure the policy prevented, and would have cost the bottom decile
\pounds\MsEnergyGrossGbpDecOne\ a year, \MsEnergyGrossPctSpendDecOne\
per cent of its spending. Three labelled qualifications bound that number, and each is a
different kind of bound. First, \emph{welfare versus cash}: the
fixed-basket figure is the first-order welfare measure, correct to a
first approximation by the envelope theorem, but households cut
consumption, so cash outlays rose by less. Applying the observed
weather-adjusted reduction in domestic gas consumption of
\MsEnergyObservedDemandFallPct\ per cent over the gas year to mid-May
2023 \citep{desnz2024} gives a cash-outlay burden of
\MsEnergyCashOutlayScalePct\ per cent of the fixed-basket figure
(the gas-only response is applied to the combined electricity and gas
bill; electricity demand fell by less, so the scale slightly
overstates the outlay reduction)
(\pounds\MsEnergyCashOutlayGbpDecOne\ for the bottom decile;
\pounds\MsEnergyCashOutlayAggBn\ billion aggregate). The two answer
different questions, what households paid, and what they lost, and
the gap between them is itself the foregone consumption. Second, \emph{how much of this is trade}: on Ofgem's published
component breakdown \citep{ofgem2022}, wholesale energy accounts for
\MsEnergyWholesaleShareOfRisePct\ per cent of the cap's rise, so about
\pounds\MsEnergyRealisedTradeTransmittedAggBn\ billion of the realised
burden is exposure to a world-market-linked price
(Appendix~\ref{app:wholesale}, which also sets out why wholesale is not
the same as imported). Third,
\emph{timing}: the counterfactual averages the April and October 2022
caps over the financial year; a variant uses the January 2023 cap that
Ofgem actually set for the final quarter
(Appendix~\ref{sec:results-grid}). Both weight the half-years equally,
although most gas is consumed in the half-year the Guarantee covered,
so both understate the Guarantee's share. Because both paths are
financial-year means, the \pounds\MsEnergyCushionAggSixMonthsBn\
billion of Guarantee absorption on the decile-table base (the second
stage's household base puts it at \pounds\SsEpgBn\ billion,
reconciled in Appendix~\ref{app:recon}) already
represents the instrument's six months at annual spending; the OBR's \pounds\MsEnergyObrEpgBn\
billion lifetime costing (March 2023) is a context anchor on different
prices and a longer window. Within those bounds the shape is
robust: the Energy Price Guarantee truncated the rise to
$+$\MsEnergyNetFactorPct\ per cent, absorbing
\pounds\MsEnergyCushionGbpDecOne\ a year for a bottom-decile
household, \MsEnergyCushionShareFyPct\ per cent of the gross shock on
the financial-year basis (\pounds\SsEpgBn\ of \pounds\SsCounterAggBn\
billion, Section~\ref{sec:results-secondstage}); the point-to-point
cap ratio, $(3{,}549-2{,}500)/(3{,}549-1{,}277)$, is
\MsEnergyCushionSharePct\ per cent and is not used further.
Because the EPG capped the \emph{price}, its cushioning share is
constant across deciles by construction. Decile means understate the dispersion the instrument faced;
\citet{levell2025} show that a price subsidy can form part of an
optimal package when income predicts exposure poorly
(Section~\ref{sec:framework-decile}). The
finding here is descriptive: the cap cushioned proportionally. Whether the overall
discretionary response was progressive cannot be judged from the EPG
alone: the Energy Bills Support Scheme's flat \pounds 400, the
means-tested cost-of-living payments and the council-tax rebate, jointly of the same order as the EPG and deliberately targeted, are
not in the decile-table analysis. Section~\ref{sec:results-secondstage}
reports the household-level result: with the whole modelled stack,
the cushioning rate falls with income.



\paragraph{The US tariffs.} The UK consumer side is zero, no
retaliation occurred, so this episode's incidence is entirely
earnings-led: a gross loss of \pounds\MsUSTariffGrossShockMillions\
million a year, of which the tax--benefit system absorbs
\MsUSTariffDisplacementCushionPct\ per cent when the loss arrives as
displacement (standard deviation
\MsUSTariffDisplacementCushionSDPct\ points across 20 assignment
draws) and \MsUSTariffWageCutCushionPct\ per cent when it arrives as
broad wage cuts (three draws; that scenario is deterministic up to
Universal Credit take-up). The calculation uses the earnings-channel
model of \citet{ahmadi2026} on the same Microcosm UK records and model
version as the price episodes, under 2026--27 rules. The lower rate
under displacement is consistent with how the two margins meet the
system: a wage cut is shared with the Exchequer at the worker's
marginal rate of income tax and National Insurance and, for Universal
Credit recipients, at the taper, whereas a displaced worker loses the
whole wage and receives flat-rate out-of-work support that replaces a
small share of typical earnings.

\paragraph{The India gains.} Of the \pounds\MsCetaTotalMillions\ million of final-goods duty
reductions, \pounds\MsCetaAllocatedMillions\ million falls in the
three categories with published spending detail (clothing, footwear,
food and beverages) and is allocated across deciles; the remainder is
unmodelled. At full pass-through the India CETA duty cuts are worth \pounds\MsCetaGainGbpPtHundredDecOne\ a year
to a bottom-decile household and \pounds\MsCetaGainGbpPtHundredDecTen\
to a top-decile one, \MsCetaVerdict\ as a share of spending, though
larger in pounds at the top (the top decile gains about three times as much in
cash), and economically negligible at any pass-through assumption
(\pounds\MsCetaGainGbpPtFiftyDecOne--\MsCetaGainGbpPtFiftyDecTen\ at
50 per cent).

\section{The second stage: household-level results}\label{sec:results-secondstage}

\subsection{Method}\label{sec:secondstage-method}

The household-level analysis uses PolicyEngine UK, an open-source
static microsimulation model that encodes UK income tax, National
Insurance and the main benefits as dated statutory parameters, in the
tax--benefit microsimulation tradition surveyed by
\citet{figari2015}. It is run through policyengine.py 6.20.0
(PolicyEngine UK \SsPolicyEngineVersion) on the certified Microcosm UK
2024--25 dataset, \SsHouseholdsRecords\ household records derived from the Family
Resources Survey, with imputations from the Wealth and Assets Survey,
the Living Costs and Food Survey (LCFS) and the Survey of Personal
Incomes, synthetic support records, and calibrated grossing weights.
The records descend from about 16,300 source survey households, the
unit the bootstrap below resamples. Households, weights and nominal incomes come from the
certified Microcosm UK 2024--25 dataset, held at 2024--25 values and
evaluated under 2023--24 (and, for the uprating comparison, 2022--23)
rules; because energy and total spending are rebased to FY2021--22,
the microdata serve as a distributional template. The tariff episode
runs on the same records and model version under 2026--27 rules. The
following conventions govern the numbers below, and each is a choice
a reader could make differently.

\emph{One basis for both paths.} The counterfactual and realised energy
paths are both financial-year mean caps: the FY2022--23 counterfactual
is \pounds\SsCounterFyCap\ (the April and October 2022 caps, no
Guarantee), the realised path \pounds\SsRealisedFyCap\ (April 2022 cap
then the Guarantee), and the FY2021--22 baseline
\pounds\SsBaseFyCap. This matters: comparing a point-to-point cap ratio
with a financial-year mean, as is tempting, silently changes the
denominator of the cushioning rate.

\emph{Rebasing (assumption).} The dataset's energy spending is at the price levels of its 2024--25 base year, so its level reflects later prices while the cross-household distribution reflects quantities. The
weighted mean is rebased to the FY2021--22 mean cap, a factor of
\SsRebaseFactor, preserving each household's relative position. Every
shock aggregate below is linear in this factor and should be read as
conditional on it; the within-decile results are invariant to it. The
cushioning \emph{rates} are not: the flat-cash instruments (the Bills
Support Scheme, the cost-of-living payments) do not scale with the
rebase while the shock denominator does, so the discretionary rate and
the decile cushioning shares depend on the factor. The dependence is
quantified in the two-axis grid of Table~\ref{tab:grid}.

\emph{Rule year and data year (assumption).} The episode is
FY2022--23, the simulation scores it under 2023--24 rules, and the
households, weights and nominal incomes are those of 2024--25, copied
unchanged into the simulated year. The rulebook-vintage run below
scores the same households under the 2022--23 parameter system as the
crisis-year counterfactual; the discretionary-stack results are
insensitive to the rule year because the instruments enter as declared
cash amounts, and the cost-of-living eligibility flags are nearly
identical under the two vintages
(\SsVinColHouseholdsRuleTwentyTwoM\ against
\SsVinColHouseholdsRuleTwentyThreeM\ million flagged households).

\emph{Joint error (indicative).} The disclosed mismatches, 2024--25
incomes and weights for a 2022--23 episode, an FYE2022 spending base,
grossing weights \SsWeightExcessPct\ per cent above the ONS count, and
the gap between the household-level and decile-table aggregates
(Appendix~\ref{app:recon}), mean that levels in this section are indicative, with an error band that is not computed here.
Figures are reported to three digits for reproducibility, not as
claimed accuracy.

\emph{Exposure, coverage and weights.} Exposure is the model's
domestic energy consumption. The Guarantee enters as the difference
between the two capped paths, the Energy Bills Support Scheme as its
flat \pounds 400 to every weighted household, and the 2022--23
cost-of-living payments on the model's own 2022--23 eligibility rules; the council-tax
rebate and the Warm Home Discount are not modelled. Deciles are of
equivalised HBAI before-housing-costs income, and burdens are shares
of household expenditure, with numerator and denominator both rebased
to FY2021--22 (the expenditure factor is \SsSpendRebase). The
household grossing weights total \SsWeightedHouseholdsM\ million
against an ONS count of \SsOnsHouseholdsM\ million, so aggregates sit
about \SsWeightExcessPct\ per cent above an ONS-count basis; ratios
are unaffected. Appendix~\ref{app:conventions} gives each convention
in full and Appendix~\ref{app:recon} reconciles the aggregates.


\subsection{No within-year automatic response to a price shock}

Every means-tested award in the UK conditions on nominal income, and a
price shock leaves nominal income untouched, so the fourteen cash
benefits cannot respond within the year: the zero is a property of
the statutory rules and of the fixed nominal inputs, not an empirical
finding. The tax--benefit calculator is run twice, once on baseline
inputs and once with energy inputs shocked to the realised path, and
the change across the fourteen benefits is \pounds\SsAutomaticBn\
billion (\SsAutomaticRatePct\ per cent of the shock). The model's own
Energy Price Guarantee variable does read energy consumption; the
paper classifies the Guarantee as discretionary and models it on the
financial-year basis of Section~\ref{sec:secondstage-method}, and
re-ranking deciles on income net of the model's native support
instruments leaves the gradient intact (Appendix~\ref{app:native}).
The zero is also a
first-order claim: a shock of this size moved nominal incomes in
general equilibrium, wage bargaining, self-employment margins, and
those excluded channels (Section~\ref{sec:framework}) would trigger
means-tested responses the first-order calculation cannot. This is consistent with \citet{cohenfollette2000}, who find in a
macro model that automatic stabilisers do little against a supply
shock.

Two qualifications bound the claim. It is a statement about the
\emph{modelled} statutory system, so channels outside it, the Warm
Home Discount, capital drawdown altering means-tested entitlement,
passported eligibility shifting at the margin, self-employed income
responses, could move the true figure off zero, though all are small
relative to the shock. And it is an in-year statement: indexation is
the system's automatic price-side channel, and it arrives late rather
than never.

\subsection{Indexation arrives late}\label{sec:results-indexation}

Benefits rose 3.1 per cent in April 2022, indexed to the previous
September, against inflation over the year of about 10 per cent, the
benefit-erosion mechanism whose cross-national treatment is
\citet{paulus2020}. The
cost of that gap is here computed with a genuine rulebook-vintage
run rather than a flat base-times-gap multiplication: the dataset carries inputs for a single period, so every input column is copied to the 2022 and 2023 periods and the \emph{same} households are scored under the
2022 and 2023 parameter systems. Scoring the same households under the 2023
parameter system instead of the 2022 system moves the fourteen cash
benefits from \pounds\SsVinBenefitBaseRuleTwentyTwo\ to
\pounds\SsVinBenefitBaseRuleTwentyThree\ billion:
\pounds\SsVinUpratingEffectBn\ billion through the full statutory
structure, tapers included. The fourteen include the state pension,
the largest single item, so a large part of the figure is pensioner
benefits rather than working-age awards. The April 2023 uprating (10.1 per cent,
applied to thirteen of the fourteen; the winter fuel payment is a
fixed cash amount) is the largest change between the two systems but
not the only one: the benefit cap and Universal Credit work allowances
rose under the same order, the Universal Credit childcare caps rose in
June 2023, and the National Insurance primary threshold rose in July
2022, so \pounds\SsVinPerPointBn\ billion per uprating point is a
calibration that a parameters-only reform would refine. At that rate,
the \SsUpratingGapPp\ points by which the April 2022 uprating fell
short of the September 2022 inflation rate correspond to
\pounds\SsVinLagCostBn\ billion over the crisis year, an infeasible, perfect-foresight
benchmark, since the September 2022 rate was not known in April 2022
(the final step
is linear in the rate; the non-uprating parameter changes in the
two-vintage difference pull in both directions, the benefit cap, work
allowances and childcare caps upward and the July 2022 National
Insurance threshold downward, so the sign of the resulting bias is
not determined), against the roughly
\pounds 12 billion aggregate real fall of \citet{obrwelfare2022} on a
different base and counterfactual, and below the
\pounds\SsUpratingShortfallBn\ billion a flat multiplication of the
2022--23-rules base excluding the winter fuel payment
(\pounds\SsBenefitIncomeBn\ billion) would give; the difference
reflects the interaction of the uprating with tapers and caps and the
other parameter changes in the two-vintage comparison. For a single adult on Universal Credit,
the standard allowance fell \pounds\MsUcLagCostGbp\ short of
month-by-month CPI indexation over 2022--23
(Appendix~\ref{sec:results-rulebook}).
It is deliberately \emph{not} expressed as a share of the energy
shock: the numerator is the erosion of benefit income by all inflation,
and dividing it by one commodity's counterfactual would attribute to
energy what food, services and rents also did. What the number
establishes is narrower: the one automatic price-side channel,
indexation, delivered its April 2022 step below the inflation of the
year in which the shock landed.

\subsection{The discretionary response by income decile}

Against a counterfactual shock of \pounds\SsCounterAggBn\ billion
(\SsCounterRisePct\ per cent on the FY basis), the
\emph{energy-specific} instruments, the Guarantee
(\pounds\SsEpgBn\ billion) and the Bills Support Scheme
(\pounds\SsEbssBn\ billion), returned \SsGridPaperEbssRatePct\ per
cent (\SsGridPaperEbssDOne\ per cent at the bottom decile,
\SsGridPaperEbssDTen\ at the top). Adding the cost-of-living payments
(\pounds\SsColBn\ billion) takes the full stack to
\pounds\SsDiscretionaryBn\ billion, or \SsDiscretionaryRatePct\ per
cent. Two attributions are reported: the cost-of-living
payments were legislated against the general inflation episode, not
energy specifically, so dividing them by the energy-only
counterfactual over-attributes to this shock by the same logic on
which the preceding subsection does not divide the indexation
shortfall by the energy shock. On either attribution the rate falls
with income: on the full stack,
\SsCushionPctDecOne\ per cent of the bottom decile's counterfactual
loss was returned against \SsCushionPctDecTen\ per cent of the top
decile's, because flat payments are worth more relative to a small
bill and the cost-of-living payments concentrate in the lower deciles. Two
features of the profile need noting. First, no
decile's rate reaches 100 per cent: the bottom decile's
\SsCushionPctDecOne\ per cent exists only on the full-stack
attribution, and it compares the instruments with the counterfactual
energy cost alone, not with inflation generally, against which the
preceding subsection shows those households were not made whole.
Second, the gradient has one non-monotone step: the third decile
(\SsCushionPctDecThree\ per cent) sits above the second
(\SsCushionPctDecTwo).
 Adding the Bills Support Scheme and the cost-of-living payments
changes the pattern from the Guarantee's constant rate to rates that
fall with income. Figure~\ref{fig:decomp} shows the anatomy in
pounds: the counterfactual shock by decile, split into the part the
household bore and what each instrument returned. A household bootstrap
(\SsBootReps\ resamples clustered on the source survey household, following the practice
urged for microsimulation results by \citet{goedeme2013}) puts a 95 per cent
sampling interval of \SsRateLo--\SsRateHi\ per cent around the
\SsDiscretionaryRatePct\ per cent aggregate rate. That interval
bounds sampling noise, not specification error, and is narrower than
the disclosed modelling approximations. The largest approximation is
testable: recalibrating the EBSS to its
\pounds\SsEbssOutturnBn\ billion NAO outturn moves the full-stack
rate from \SsDiscretionaryRatePct\ to \SsCalFullRatePct\ per cent,
so the headline survives it. The same bootstrap puts
\SsCushionDecOneLo--\SsCushionDecOneHi\ per cent around the bottom
decile's \SsCushionPctDecOne, an interval that excludes 100 per cent.
The net borne amount is positive in deciles one, two, four and five
and spans zero only in the third (95 per cent intervals
\pounds\SsNetLoDecOne--\SsNetHiDecOne,
\pounds\SsNetLoDecTwo--\SsNetHiDecTwo,
\pounds\SsNetLoDecThree--\SsNetHiDecThree,
\pounds\SsNetLoDecFour--\SsNetHiDecFour\ and
\pounds\SsNetLoDecFive--\SsNetHiDecFive\ a year for deciles one to
five). These
are sampling intervals over the survey base only: the resampling does
not re-calibrate the grossing weights or reflect the survey's design,
and imputation uncertainty from the consumption donor models is not
captured (Section~\ref{sec:results-withindec}).

\subsection{Sensitivity to the rebase and the price path}

\subsection{Imputed exposure varies mostly within deciles}\label{sec:results-withindec}

Figure~\ref{fig:paths} plots the three shock paths of the design on
one distribution: the gross counterfactual, the realised path, and the
cash-outlay path (the gross counterfactual scaled by the observed
demand response, the same convention as the decile-table analysis). By construction the three paths are scalar multiples, so they differ in level, not shape.
The realised burden runs from \SsBurdenPctSpendDecOne\ per cent of
household expenditure in the bottom decile to
\SsBurdenPctSpendDecTen\ per cent in the top. The gradient is regressive and essentially monotone (the third and fourth deciles are level).
On the unified base the levels can be compared with their decile-table
counterparts (\SsBurdenPctSpendDecOne\ against
\MsEnergyRealisedPctSpendDecOne\ per cent at the bottom decile), and
energy exposure varies within income deciles as well as between them.
Decomposing the variance of that burden puts
\SsBetweenPct\ per cent of it between deciles and
\SsWithinPct\ per cent within them on the paper's preferred basis
(expenditure share, trimmed at the first and ninety-ninth percentiles;
household-bootstrap 95 per cent interval
\SsWithinLo--\SsWithinHi\ per cent for the within-share);
untrimmed the between-share is the same, \SsBetweenPctUntrimmed\ per
cent, because the imputed shares have no extreme tails, and in logs it
is \SsBetweenPctLog\ per cent.
 On every
basis, though, the gradient the decile-table analysis measured is a small part of
the dispersion in who was exposed. Within the bottom decile alone the burden runs from
\SsPTenDecOne\ per cent of spending at the tenth percentile to
\SsPNinetyDecOne\ per cent at the ninetieth.

Two limits bound what this establishes. The exposure is
\emph{imputed}, the dataset's energy spending is imputed from LCFS donors, so part of the within-decile dispersion is donor-model variation, and
the decomposition describes the imputed exposure without establishing
that it bounds the true dispersion, the bootstrap interval above is
sampling uncertainty only, blind to the donor-model error, and a
direct validation of the imputed within-decile spread against the
LCFS donor sample, which observes it, is left to future work; \citet{levell2025} use panel
data on household energy spending for this reason. And the estimand is the variance of one
commodity's expenditure share, not the variance of welfare changes from
product-level trade exposure, which is what
\citet{borusyakjaravel2024} decompose: the results point the same way,
but they are not the same quantity.

\subsection{A second episode through the second stage: the TCA food shock}\label{sec:results-tca-ss}

The common cushioning estimand is computed for a second episode, and
this one is a trade-\emph{policy} act. The TCA food-price first stage
(the window-average path of Section~\ref{sec:results}) is priced
against each household's imputed food consumption on the same
\SsHouseholdsRecords\ records, rebased so the weighted mean matches
the decile-table ONS FYE2022 food-spend base (factor
\SsTcaSsFoodRebase; the same convention as the energy episode's cap
rebase, with shares on the same rebased spending base). The aggregate,
\pounds\SsTcaSsAggBn\ billion a year, therefore reproduces the decile-table analysis up to the grossing-weight excess; the new content of this stage
is the distribution, not the level.
The burden is regressive (\SsTcaSsPctSpendDecOne\ per cent of
bottom-decile spending against \SsTcaSsPctSpendDecTen\ at the top,
against the decile-table \MsTcaWindowAvgPctSpendDecOne\ and
\MsTcaWindowAvgPctSpendDecTen, close at both ends;
the pound figures differ little from the decile-table ones,
a distributional divergence of the consumption imputation worth
noting beside Section~\ref{sec:results-withindec}'s caveats) and larger in pounds at the top
(\pounds\SsTcaSsGbpDecOne\ at the bottom against
\pounds\SsTcaSsGbpDecTen\ at the top), and \SsTcaSsWithinPct\ per cent of the variance in exposure
lies within deciles (trimmed basis; within the bottom decile the
burden runs \SsTcaSsPTenDecOne--\SsTcaSsPNinetyDecOne\ per cent of
spending at the tenth to ninetieth percentile). The cushioning
decomposition is short: no modelled entitlement reads food
consumption, so the cash-benefit response is zero by construction;
indexation ran late, as above; and no episode-specific instrument exists, nothing was
legislated against the TCA food-price effect. The contrast with the
energy episode must be drawn on the episode-specific basis, by the
same attribution logic as the discretionary subsection above: the
general cost-of-living payments, which the episode-specific
attribution excludes from the energy stack, also cushioned food-price
inflation in 2022--23, so they cannot count towards energy and as zero
for food. Episode-specific,
the contrast is the paper's central comparative fact: against the
energy shock the state legislated instruments returning
\SsGridPaperEbssRatePct\ per cent of the counterfactual loss; against
the food shock, of the same sign and a similar spending-share
gradient, it legislated none. The two episodes differed in size (the realised household-level energy aggregate is about \SsEnergyToTcaRatio\ times the window-average food aggregate, and a smaller multiple of its end-state rate) and in speed; the paper does not estimate why
policymakers responded to one and not the other.

\subsection{The India agreement on household records}\label{sec:results-ceta-ss}

The projected gains are carried to the same records. The declared
duty reductions (\pounds\MsCetaClothingMillions\ million on clothing, \pounds\MsCetaFootwearMillions\ million on footwear and \pounds\MsCetaFoodBevMillions\ million on food and non-alcoholic beverages) are spread
across households in proportion to their imputed category spending,
at the declared 100 per cent pass-through and a 50 per cent variant.
At full pass-through the mean gain runs from
\pounds\SsCetaSsGbpDecOne\ a year in the bottom decile to
\pounds\SsCetaSsGbpDecTen\ in the top
(\pounds\SsCetaSsGbpFiftyDecOne--\pounds\SsCetaSsGbpFiftyDecTen\
at 50 per cent): larger in pounds at the top, near-proportional as a
share of spending (\SsCetaSsPctSpendDecOne\ against
\SsCetaSsPctSpendDecTen\ per cent), and small everywhere. The
within-decile share of the variance in the gain,
\SsCetaSsWithinPct\ per cent on the trimmed basis, matches the
pattern of the two negative episodes: category spending, not income,
determines who is exposed, in either direction. There is no
cushioning side to compute: gains trigger no statutory response, and
no offsetting instrument exists.
