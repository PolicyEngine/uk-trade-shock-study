\opening{Dear Editors,}

I am submitting the manuscript ``Who pays for trade shocks?
Distributional incidence and fiscal cushioning in the UK'' for
consideration in \Journal.

The paper compares, on one household microdata set and one
tax--benefit model, how the costs and gains of four UK trade-related
shocks of 2021--26 were spread across households by income
(the post-Brexit rise in food prices, the 2022 energy price surge, the
2025 US tariffs and the UK--India agreement) and how much of each the
state cushioned. Three findings stand out:

\begin{itemize}
\item Energy-specific support returned \SsEnergySpecificRateMinPct--\SsEnergySpecificRateMaxPct\ per cent of the
counterfactual energy cost, and \SsFullRateMinPct--\SsFullRateMaxPct\ per cent with the Cost of
Living Payments; no instrument was legislated against the food-price
rise.
\item The one automatic price-side channel, benefit indexation, arrived
a year late: matching the April 2022 uprating to that year's inflation
would have raised benefit income by \pounds\SsLagReformBn\ billion, more than the
Cost of Living Payments in every income decile, narrowly at the bottom.
\item In a US-tariff earnings scenario, taxes and benefits offset
\EThreeDispTaxBenRatePct--\EThreeWageTaxBenRatePct\ per cent of lost earnings, a property of the UK system
rather than of the tariff, and the loss falls on the middle and top of
the distribution rather than the bottom.
\end{itemize}

The contribution is to place price and earnings shocks from trade on
one incidence and cushioning metric, linking the trade-incidence
literature to work on automatic stabilisers and benefit indexation.

The manuscript is not under consideration elsewhere and has not been
published. Thank you for considering it.

\closing{Yours sincerely,}
