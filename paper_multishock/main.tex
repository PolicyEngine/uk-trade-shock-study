\begin{abstract}
\noindent Between 2021 and 2026 Britain experienced five trade-related
shocks: the new customs border with the EU (2021), an energy price
surge transmitted through world markets (2022), US tariffs on British
exports (2025), and two new trade agreements (India, 2026; CPTPP,
2024). This paper asks the same question of all five: once each shock
reached households, who bore it, and how much did the tax--benefit
system give back? The exercise is policy simulation, not causal
estimation: the size of each shock is taken from published estimates
or official projections, and the results are conditional on those
declared inputs. The contribution is to trace them
to households---with decile-level spending data for the three price
episodes (the tariff episode has no UK consumer-price channel, and
CPTPP no distributional structure), and a full tax--benefit
simulation (PolicyEngine UK, on
\SsHouseholdsRecords\ survey households weighted to represent the
population) for the two largest. Three findings. First, the two
harmful price shocks---dearer food after Brexit, dearer energy in
2022---hit poorer households hardest, because food and energy take a
larger share of their budgets; the projected gains from the new
agreements are small and spread evenly. Second, the benefit system
offers no automatic protection against price shocks by the design of
its rules: British benefits respond when incomes fall, not when
prices rise, and the annual inflation uprating lagged a year
behind---a delay worth roughly \pounds 12--15 billion to benefit
recipients in the crisis year (the paper's rulebook-run figure is
\pounds\SsVinLagCostBn\ billion; the OBR's, on a different basis,
about \pounds 12 billion). What protected households from the energy shock was emergency
policy: across the paper's sensitivity grid the energy-specific
instruments returned roughly half to three-fifths of the shock
(three-fifths to just over three-quarters counting the general
cost-of-living support), more of it to poorer households than to richer ones. The
Brexit food shock, just as regressive, had no instrument aimed at
it. Feeding the measured incidence into an MPC-weighted first-round
demand calculation shows the support was also demand-efficient---the
targeted payments protected the most consumption per pound---and that
the year-late uprating withheld income from the highest-MPC
households in the economy. Third, on the imputed spending data,
income is a poor guide to
exposure: around nine-tenths of the variation in how hard households
were hit lies \emph{within} income deciles, not between them, which
is why income-targeted support struggles to reach the
worst-affected.
\end{abstract}
