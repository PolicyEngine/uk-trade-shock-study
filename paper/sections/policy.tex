\section{Policy}\label{sec:policy}

\subsection{Economic Prosperity Deal}\label{sec:policy-epd}

Within the maintained direct-channel scenarios, the paired full-minus-EPD displacement contrast is $\EPDWorkerDifferenceThousands\pm\EPDWorkerDifferenceSDThousands$ thousand workers, \pounds $\EPDGrossDifference\pm\EPDGrossDifferenceSD$ million of gross earnings and \pounds $\EPDExchequerDifference\pm\EPDExchequerDifferenceSD$ million of annual Exchequer cost. On every one of those three quantities the assignment dispersion is as large as, or larger than, the difference itself, so this section does not price the deal: at the draw count run here the paired contrast cannot be distinguished from zero even on the narrow criterion of Monte Carlo allocation, before any question of causal interpretation arises. The point estimates are reported because the sign and the channel are informative---autos and the pharmaceutical exemption drive the difference, and the scenario applies the in-quota rate to fixed exposure without modelling quota allocation or future above-quota trade---but a reader should take nothing quantitative from them. Raising the draw count for this contrast specifically, which is cheap relative to the rest of the design, is the obvious remedy and has not been done here. The whole section rests, in addition, on a sector calibration that its own rank-order check fails to corroborate (Section~\ref{sec:results-benchmarks}); it is retained as a demonstration of the machinery rather than as a policy evaluation.

\subsection{Pharmaceutical channel}\label{sec:policy-pharma}

The EPD scenario sets the pharmaceutical employment shock to zero, but this does not make the exemption free. Its VPAG and NHS pricing concessions sit outside the labour-income microsimulation. A complete policy appraisal would cost those health-budget and price effects alongside the direct tax--benefit scenario.

\subsection{Cushioning policy}\label{sec:policy-reforms}

The simulations support a limited conclusion: extensive-margin protection depends more on means-tested eligibility and claiming than the income-tax response to an intensive earnings loss. The take-up experiments sharpen where the policy question sits. The new-entitlement claiming rate is not the binding parameter---indeed it binds on nobody, since almost every displaced household is already UC-entitled in work---so the leverage lies entirely with the gates this model holds fixed or cannot exercise. Three matter, and they do not point the same way. Claiming among long-standing eligible non-claimants is worth about \TakeupEntitledSpread\ percentage points of displacement cushioning (on the source and calibration set out in Section~\ref{sec:results-takeup}), and the absence of New Style JSA a further \JSACushionPoints\ points: both mean the model \emph{understates} the protection a displaced worker would receive. The unmodelled capital test---redundancy payments against thresholds frozen since 2006---runs the other way and means it overstates protection. The net sign is therefore not established, and the policy question is which of these gates binds hardest in practice rather than how large the modelled cushion is. Automatic enrolment after redundancy, contribution-based unemployment insurance and wage insurance are therefore candidate counterfactuals for future analysis, not policy recommendations established by the present results. Their costs and incidence require dedicated simulations and financing assumptions.

The repository also supplies reusable interfaces for the next policy exercises.
Observable household and worker characteristics can tilt shock probabilities
while preserving each sector's aggregate earnings loss, and a separate
price-channel interface converts expenditure-weighted price changes into real
disposable income. Neither interface supplies causal coefficients: the former
requires externally estimated heterogeneity and the latter requires household
expenditure data. They are extensions to populate and validate, not additional
headline estimates in this paper.
