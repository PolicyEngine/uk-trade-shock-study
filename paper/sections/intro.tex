\section{Introduction}\label{sec:intro}

Who bears a trade shock? When a tariff falls on a country's exports, the
loss reaches households through the labour market, and the same aggregate
loss in employee earnings can arrive by very different routes: as smaller pay
packets for people who keep their jobs, or as the complete loss of earnings
for a smaller number who do not. This paper asks how much of that loss the UK
tax--benefit system absorbs, and how much the answer depends on which route
the loss takes.

The question is worth posing because the answer is not invariant, and the
reason it is not invariant is the nonlinearity of the schedule itself. For an
equal aggregate loss, a broad reduction in earnings leaves most households in
work and inside the tax schedule, where each lost pound is relieved at a
marginal rate. A loss concentrated on a few workers removes their earnings
entirely, exhausting that marginal-rate relief and drawing instead on
means-tested support that a partial cut never triggers. The two are not the
same experiment even when the aggregate is identical, and the difference
between them is a statement about the tax schedule rather than about
unemployment.

That distinction has a direct bearing on how trade-shock incidence is usually
measured. The microsimulation literature on automatic stabilisers---%
\citet{dolls2012} for nineteen EU countries and the United States, and
\citet{figari2011}, \citet{fernandezsalgado2014} and
\citet{brewertasseva2021} after them---reports a coefficient for a
proportional income shock beside one for a rise in unemployment, and the
difference is read as the extensive margin. It is not identified as one: the
two shocks also differ in how concentrated the loss is, so the comparison
mixes a concentration effect with a margin effect and reports the sum under
the margin's name. Anyone using those coefficients to price the household
incidence of a trade shock inherits that conflation.

The application is the 2025 United States tariff schedule as it falls on UK
goods exports. The tariff calculation sets the size of the aggregate
employee-earnings loss and its distribution across industry divisions; it is
a calibration of the shock, not a causal estimate of tariff transmission, and
Section~\ref{sec:results-benchmarks} is explicit about how much corroboration
it does and does not receive from outturn data.

The design separates the two. Holding the aggregate employee-earnings loss fixed---roughly
\pounds\FullWageGross\,million a year in the application, calibrated from the
2025 US tariff schedule---I vary only its incidence across Family Resources
Survey households in PolicyEngine UK: diffuse proportional cuts across all
exposed workers; a concentrated cut imposing a displacement draw's exact
worker-level losses with no job loss; and displacement of those same workers.
Defining the cushioning rate as $1-\Delta Y/\Delta E$, where $\Delta E$ is the
gross employee-earnings loss and $\Delta Y$ the household disposable-income
loss, diffuse cuts are cushioned at \SubmissionUnitWageCushion\ per cent and
displacement at
$\SubmissionUnitDisplacedCushion \pm \SubmissionUnitDisplacedCushionSD$ per
cent. The middle cell splits that gap: \FactorialConcStep\ points come from
concentrating the loss on selected workers at fixed employment state, and
\FactorialStateStep\ points from the employment-state change itself
(Section~\ref{sec:results-factorial}).

The near-zero state component is the point, and it requires care to state.
It is \emph{not} a general property of annual static microsimulation. EUROMOD
and UKMOD simulate contributory unemployment benefit from constructed
earnings and contribution histories---the Labour Market Adjustment add-on
exists to build exactly those characteristics for people moved into
unemployment---and \citet{figari2011} impose employment-to-unemployment
transitions and find the entitlement rules bite heterogeneously, with around
73 per cent of the newly unemployed qualifying in the United Kingdom against
62 per cent in Italy. A rule that denies two in five claimants is not a
label. \citet{dolls2012} likewise recover a substantial out-of-work benefit
response---0.163 of their UK unemployment coefficient against 0.031 for the
income shock---from an annual static model, by imposing their shock through
reweighting rather than through a rule-based transition.

The near-zero step here is a property of \emph{this pipeline}. PolicyEngine
UK carries New Style Jobseeker's Allowance as a survey-reported variable
rather than a simulated rule, so an imposed change of
\texttt{employment\_status} triggers no contributory entitlement; the stored
claiming flag is frozen at its pre-shock draw; and no monthly assessment,
waiting period or conditionality exists in an annual model to be triggered.
The two cells being compared therefore differ, in this implementation, only
by a label no active formula reads. To fix the order of magnitude of what is
missing: New Style JSA alone would be worth up to
\JSACushionPoints\ percentage points of cushioning for a representative
displaced worker, tens of times the step the decomposition records.

The consequence is a warning about implementation rather than about a class
of model. A stabilisation contrast computed on a pipeline that leaves
status-contingent rules inactive measures loss concentration and reports it
under the employment margin's name. Whether any particular published estimate
is affected depends on whether its pipeline simulates those rules---which the
add-ons above show is a choice, not a constraint---and that is a question the
literature does not currently ask of itself.

The concentration channel is itself a statement about tax progressivity. A
single-worker calculation on the exposed-sector mean settles its sign without
a microsimulation---\ScheduleMarginalRate\ per cent marginal against
\ScheduleAverageRate\ per cent average, a wedge of
\ScheduleImpliedGap\ points---but not its magnitude. Weighted as the
comparison actually is---by the wage bill, over a population more
concentrated above the higher-rate threshold than the exposed-sector mean of
\pounds 48{,}272 suggests---income tax and employee National Insurance relieve
\WageChannelTaxAndNI\ per cent of a diffuse loss against
\ConcChannelTaxAndNI\ per cent of a concentrated one, a tax-and-National-%
Insurance wedge of \ScheduleWedgeWeighted\ points, against a simulated step of
\FactorialConcStepChannelSeeds. Those four figures are the channel split,
which runs on \FactorialChannelSeeds\ common seeds rather than the
\SubmissionDraws\ assignments behind the cushioning rates above; the two
bases are not interchangeable and Section~\ref{sec:results-factorial}
quantifies the difference. The household system therefore \emph{works
against} the schedule, subtracting \ScheduleWedgeOffsets\ points, most of
them Universal Credit responding to a complete loss as a marginal cut never
triggers it. Identifying that offset is what the microsimulation contributes;
the sign and order of magnitude were settled by the schedule.

The gap is
not an artefact of the HBAI treatment of pension saving: recomputed under a
pension-gross income concept, both margins' cushioning falls by four to five
percentage points and the wage-minus-displacement contrast widens slightly,
from \GapHBAIPension\ to \GapPensionGross\ points
(Section~\ref{sec:results-pension}).

The scale of the exercise should be clear from the outset, because it governs
which results can carry weight. That fixed loss is borne under displacement by
about \FullDisplacedWorkers\ thousand represented workers on the
\ProductionDraws-draw comparator design---small relative to
national aggregates, and drawn from a pool of roughly a thousand exposed
Family Resources Survey records of which only about ten are selected in any
one assignment. National poverty and inequality movements are consequently
near zero everywhere in this paper and carry no information; the object of
study is the \emph{composition} of the fiscal response to a given loss, not
its aggregate size, and no subgroup, regional or constituency reading of the
displacement scenarios is supportable at this level of survey support.

The comparison matters because the UK tax--benefit system is nonlinear. For
an equal aggregate loss, a broad reduction in earnings leaves most households
in work and inside the tax schedule, whereas a concentrated loss moves
households across benefit-eligibility thresholds. Fiscal incidence therefore
depends on who loses earnings, whether the loss is temporary, and whether
employment resumes---and a single wage-cut or displacement scenario should
not be read as a forecast of realised trade adjustment. The two poles also
price a structural disagreement: flexible-wage models turn a tariff primitive
into a wage shock \citep{ignatenko2025}, while downward-nominal-rigidity
models turn the same primitive into a job shock \citep{rodriguezclare2025}.
Rather than endogenising the margin, I hold the aggregate loss fixed and
report what each pole implies for households and the Exchequer.

The epistemic status of every input is stated once, here, and in the
design table of Section~\ref{sec:method-primitives}, rather than repeated
throughout. The estimand is conditional: the statutory tax--benefit and
distributional response to a specified earnings-loss path, not a causal
estimate of the 2025 tariff episode. The 2025 US tariff schedule supplies
sector weights and shock magnitudes through a transparent accounting bridge
(tariff rate $\times$ US-export intensity $\times$ a demand calibration
$\times$ a wage-bill-incidence assumption); the bridge deliberately omits
prices, quantities, imported inputs, productivity, profits, and firm entry.
Reported $\pm$ values are assignment dispersion from the Monte Carlo
allocation of displacement, not standard errors; sensitivity of the primary
contrast to which FRS records were sampled is scored separately by a
household record-resampling exercise and a leave-one-record-out audit,
neither of which is a design-based confidence interval. Mixed-path shares
are disciplined by external evidence but not estimated.

One negative result should be on the table from the outset. A monthly HMRC
product--destination panel shows a post-policy decline in US-bound exports
concentrated in large products, but the calibration earns no credit at sector
rank: the rank correlation between predicted and realised export falls among
the ten largest exposed divisions is \RankCorrTopTen\
(Section~\ref{sec:results-benchmarks}). That figure is mechanically
degenerate rather than informative---the predictor takes only two distinct
values across the ten divisions, and the two carrying the higher value
realised the largest and the smallest falls, so their mid-ranks cancel
exactly---and with ten divisions and an underpowered product-level intensity
split, the honest reading is an absence of corroboration rather than a
decisive refutation---but the practical
consequence is the same and it bounds the paper's claims: the sector
calibration must not be read as measured tariff incidence. It does not touch
the cushioning comparison, which conditions on a declared loss rather than on
the bridge. The
leave-one-division-out audit (\LeaveSectorDraws\ assignments) across all \LeaveSectorCount\ exposed divisions
shows the wage-minus-displacement contrast surviving every rerun
(range \LeaveSectorCushionDifferenceMin--%
\LeaveSectorCushionDifferenceMax\ points): the calibration determines
\emph{who} is shocked, not the statutory asymmetry between intensive and
extensive relief.

The second stage uses the 2024--25 Family Resources Survey and PolicyEngine
UK to compute taxes, National Insurance, benefits, household disposable
income, poverty, inequality, and Exchequer effects. I report a common
aggregate loss under alternative incidence patterns, decompose the result by
tax and benefit channel, and show sensitivity to spell duration, worker
selection, benefit take-up, and re-employment. Between the polar bounds a
transition surface varies the loss share borne by workers who re-enter
services jobs after a lag; its interior points rest on assumed shares,
lags and a cross-sectional earnings penalty, so the response surface, not
any single interior calibration, is the reported object
(Section~\ref{sec:results-transition}).

The paper makes three contributions. The first is substantive and about the
United Kingdom: it prices how much of a trade-related earnings loss the
statutory system absorbs, and shows that the answer varies by
\PrimaryCushionDifference\ percentage points across delivery margins that
are indistinguishable at the aggregate level. For anyone costing the
household consequences of a tariff episode, the composition of the labour
market response is not a second-order detail---it moves the fiscal answer by
about a sixth of the loss. A corollary follows for how such exercises are
usually conducted: because the difference is loss concentration rather than
job loss, the income-shock versus unemployment-shock comparison on which the
automatic-stabiliser literature rests is a progressivity statistic reported
under an employment-margin label, and the margin component is not identified
by the class of model that produces it.

The second is institutional and specific to Universal Credit. PolicyEngine UK
carries benefit take-up as a stored benefit-unit flag drawn on pre-shock
circumstances and calibrated as a population-wide share rather than take-up
among the entitled, so a naive imposed-transition pipeline hands a displaced
worker a claiming decision made in a state of the world the shock has
destroyed. Correcting it moves displacement cushioning by
\TakeupConventionSpread\ percentage points, roughly half the headline
contrast---so the claiming convention is one of the largest single sources of
uncertainty here, not a detail. An earlier revision of this paper reported
that it changed nothing and treated that as the finding; the invariance was a
stale benefit-award cache rather than a property of Universal Credit, and is
described in Section~\ref{sec:results-takeup} because the way it presented
itself---bit-identical output across a stochastic parameter---is a trap any
imposed-transition pipeline can fall into. A second convention binds
alongside it: the frozen claiming state of the already-entitled
(Section~\ref{sec:results-takeup}). Any imposed-transition exercise in a
model of this class inherits both problems.

The third is quantitative and conditional. Two assumptions dominate the
magnitude---within-division worker selection moves the contrast by a fifth to
a third of its size, and the claiming convention moves the displacement
margin by an amount comparable to the whole contrast---while pension
accounting and duration equivalence do not. A reconciliation against the UK
cells of \citet{dolls2012}, whose ordering of income and unemployment shocks
is the reverse of the one found here, locates that discrepancy principally in
the out-of-work benefit channel (Section~\ref{sec:results-benchmarks}). The
level of the contrast is therefore not robust; its sign and mechanism are.

The remainder of the paper describes
the evidence and scenario design, presents the polar bounds and mixed paths,
reports policy counterfactuals, and discusses limitations and
reproducibility.
