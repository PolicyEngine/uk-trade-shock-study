\section{Introduction}\label{sec:intro}

Who bears an earnings loss when the same aggregate amount can be
distributed through different workers and labour-market states? This paper
answers that question for the United Kingdom. I hold an aggregate loss in
employee earnings---roughly \pounds\FullWageGross\,million a year in the
application, calibrated from the 2025 US tariff schedule---fixed, and
compare its fiscal and distributional consequences across a factorial
family of incidence patterns: diffuse proportional wage cuts across all
exposed workers, concentrated wage cuts imposing the displacement draw's
exact worker-level losses without any job loss, displacement of the same
selected workers into unemployment, and mixed transition paths with
re-employment penalties. Defining the cushioning rate as
$1-\Delta Y/\Delta E$, where $\Delta E$ is the gross employee-earnings loss
and $\Delta Y$ the household disposable-income loss, diffuse wage cuts are
cushioned at \SubmissionUnitWageCushion\ per cent while displacement is
cushioned at
$\SubmissionUnitDisplacedCushion \pm \SubmissionUnitDisplacedCushionSD$ per
cent---a gap of \PrimaryCushionDifference\ percentage points. The two
headline scenarios differ in more than the adjustment margin: they also
concentrate the loss on different workers. The factorial middle cell
separates the two channels, and the answer is one-sided. Concentrating the
same aggregate loss on the displacement draw's workers, while keeping
everyone employed, accounts for \FactorialConcStep\ points of the gap;
moving those same workers, bearing the same worker-level losses, from
employment into unemployment accounts for only the remaining
\FactorialStateStep\ points (Section~\ref{sec:results-factorial}). The
mechanism is therefore a loss-size effect inside the statutory schedule: a
diffuse in-work reduction is offset automatically at marginal income-tax
and National Insurance rates, whereas a complete earnings loss exhausts
that relief and lands on means-tested Universal Credit---whose statutory
entitlement responds to income, not employment status, so the state change
itself adds almost nothing \emph{within the modelled annual system}. The
institutions that do condition on employment status---New Style JSA,
monthly assessment periods, waiting and claiming frictions, work-search
conditionality---are exactly the ones outside the model, so the
near-zero state component is a statement about the modelled statutory
rules, not about the real-world experience of job loss. Actual receipt
additionally depends on capital holdings and claiming behaviour that the
survey data only partially observe. The gap is
not an artefact of the HBAI treatment of pension saving: recomputed under a
pension-gross income concept, both margins' cushioning falls by roughly four
percentage points but the wage-minus-displacement contrast is essentially
unchanged (Section~\ref{sec:results-cushioning}).

The comparison matters because the UK tax--benefit system is nonlinear. For
an equal aggregate loss, a broad reduction in earnings leaves most households
in work and inside the tax schedule, whereas a concentrated loss moves
households across benefit-eligibility thresholds. Fiscal incidence therefore
depends on who loses earnings, whether the loss is temporary, and whether
employment resumes---and a single wage-cut or displacement scenario should
not be read as a forecast of realised trade adjustment. The two poles also
price a structural disagreement: flexible-wage models turn a tariff primitive
into a wage shock \citep{ignatenko2025}, while downward-nominal-rigidity
models turn the same primitive into a job shock \citep{rodriguezclare2025}.
Rather than endogenising the margin, I hold the aggregate loss fixed and
report what each pole implies for households and the Exchequer.

The epistemic status of every input is stated once, here, and in the
design table of Section~\ref{sec:method-primitives}, rather than repeated
throughout. The estimand is conditional: the statutory tax--benefit and
distributional response to a specified earnings-loss path, not a causal
estimate of the 2025 tariff episode. The 2025 US tariff schedule supplies
sector weights and shock magnitudes through a transparent accounting bridge
(tariff rate $\times$ US-export intensity $\times$ a demand calibration
$\times$ a wage-bill-incidence assumption); the bridge deliberately omits
prices, quantities, imported inputs, productivity, profits, and firm entry.
Reported $\pm$ values are assignment dispersion from the Monte Carlo
allocation of displacement, not standard errors; sensitivity of the primary
contrast to which FRS records were sampled is scored separately by a
household record-resampling exercise and a leave-one-record-out audit,
neither of which is a design-based confidence interval. Mixed-path shares
are disciplined by external evidence but not estimated.

One negative result should be on the table from the outset. A monthly HMRC
product--destination panel confirms a post-policy decline in US-bound
exports, but at sector rank the calibration fails its own falsification
test: the rank correlation between predicted and realised sector export
falls among the ten largest exposed divisions is zero
(Section~\ref{sec:results-benchmarks}). This failure bounds the paper's
claims---the sector calibration must not be read as measured tariff
incidence---but it does not touch the cushioning comparison, which
conditions on a declared loss rather than on the bridge. The
leave-one-division-out audit across all \LeaveSectorCount\ exposed divisions
shows the wage-minus-displacement contrast surviving every rerun
(range \LeaveSectorCushionDifferenceMin--%
\LeaveSectorCushionDifferenceMax\ points): the calibration determines
\emph{who} is shocked, not the statutory asymmetry between intensive and
extensive relief.

The second stage uses the 2024--25 Family Resources Survey and PolicyEngine
UK to compute taxes, National Insurance, benefits, household disposable
income, poverty, inequality, and Exchequer effects. I report a common
aggregate loss under alternative incidence patterns, decompose the result by
tax and benefit channel, and show sensitivity to spell duration, worker
selection, benefit take-up, and re-employment. Between the polar bounds a
transition surface varies the loss share borne by workers who re-enter
services jobs after a lag; its interior points rest on assumed shares,
lags and a cross-sectional earnings penalty, so the response surface, not
any single interior calibration, is the reported object
(Section~\ref{sec:results-cushioning}).

The paper makes three contributions. First, it provides a reproducible UK
microsimulation framework for mapping earnings losses---here calibrated to
trade exposure as an application---into fiscal and distributional outcomes.
Second, it separates, factorially, how much of the wage-cut versus
displacement cushioning gap reflects concentrating a fixed loss on selected
workers and how much reflects the change in employment state itself.
Third, it quantifies which statutory channels drive the differences and
which assumptions---timing, take-up, pensions, and worker selection---are
most important for policy conclusions. The remainder of the paper describes
the evidence and scenario design, presents the polar bounds and mixed paths,
reports policy counterfactuals, and discusses limitations and
reproducibility.
